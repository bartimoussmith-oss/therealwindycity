The Belvoir Ranch and Big Hole Properties are a significant asset for the City of Cheyenne. The Ranch 
is located approximately eight (8) miles west of Downtown Cheyenne and continues to Harriman Road 
roughly between Interstate 80 and the Colorado border. The general location of the Belvoir Ranch can 
be seen below. The property was purchased in 2003 to be used for water development for the Board of 
Public Utilities and as a site for a landfill for the City of Cheyenne Public Works Department. The ranch 
includes approximately six (6) sections of state land on which the City is the primary leaseholder. In 
addition to the Ranch purchase of 2003, the City worked with the Nature Conservancy to acquire the Big 
Hole property. An initial Master Plan was created for the property in 2008 (including the State sections 
and the Big Hole); however, many items considered in the plan have not come to fruition. In recent years, 
significant capital has been poured into the property with the construction of over 100 wind turbines. 
The City also acquired the Mariah Pasture in 2019 adding an additional 550 acres to the Ranch in the far 
southwest corner. Furthermore, the Ranch has seen significant interest from a variety of other uses ranging 
from a variety of renewable energy alternatives to data center development. These new uses coupled 
with the implementation of trails illustrate a clear impetus for the update of the Ranch Master Plan. The 
Metropolitan Planning Organization (MPO) has also added the Belvoir Ranch to its planning area.
In March of 2024 the City of Cheyenne contracted with AVI PC to do a targeted update of the 2008 Belvoir 
Ranch and Big Hole Master Plan. The purpose of this update is to recognize the existing conditions of the 
Ranch, changes to the property since the 2008 Master Plan, and to set a framework for considering new 
uses at the Belvoir Ranch. The Plan update was crafted through significant stakeholder interaction in the 
form of public meetings and focus group discussions. These conversations helped prioritize the uses, 
goals, and policies discussed in Chapters 4 and 6. This update does not go in depth regarding existing 
conditions and the history of the Ranch, but rather presents an implementation blueprint for considering 
newly proposed uses at the Belvoir. A complete history of the Ranch can be found in the 2008 Master Plan 
as the historical information has not significantly changed. Furthermore, the 2008 Master Plan contains an 
extensive analysis of the natural and anthropological history of the Ranch.

Introduction to the Ranch
The City of Cheyenne purchased the Belvoir Ranch 
in 2003 for the purposes of developing a new landfill 
and acquiring rights to the water wells located at the 
property. The funds used to acquire the Ranch were 
a combination of funds from the Board of Public 
Utilities and the City Public Works Department, both 
of which maintain a vested interest in the Ranch for 
theses purchase reasons. The City expanded the 
size of the Ranch with the acquisition of the Big Hole 
property that borders Colorado. The purchase of this 
property was facilitated by the Nature Conservancy, 
which holds a conservation easement on it. The Big 
Hole connects to Larimer County’s Red Mountain 
Open Space which further connects to the City of 
Fort Collin’s Soapstone Prairie Natural Area.
Altogether, the Belvoir Ranch and Big Hole total 
nearly 20,000 acres, including the 4,000 acres of 
State-owned land on which the City is considered a 
lease-holder. The Ranch has always been seen as 
an opportunity for use by the City of Cheyenne, its 
residents, and other potential development.

Purchase
The City purchased the Belvoir Ranch in 2003 with the intent of a 
number of uses; including water development, a landfill site, energy 
development, and recreation. The funds used for the purchase 
were a combination of funds from Public Works and the Board of 
Public Utilities.
Big Hole 
Purchase
Two years later, the City bought the adjacent Big Hole property from 
the Nature Conservancy. The Big Hole added approximately 1,800 
acres to the Ranch and is often included in ranch planning.

In 2008, the City adopted the Belvoir Ranch Master Plan to guide 
future development of the property. The plan includes a mix 
of uses for the Belvoir, including a number of trailheads, trails, 
and connections to the adjacent Red Mountain Open Space in 
Colorado.

Acquisition 
The City of Cheyenne cooperated with Dyno Nobel to acquire the 
Mariah Pasture and conduct a land swap for the property. The 
Mariah Pasture provides additional hiking and fishing opportunities.

Priority
On January 15th , 2022, the City of Cheyenne Governing Body met 
for a goal setting session to discuss their main objectives for 2022. 
Included in this was a goal for public access on the property and 
get “boots and bikes on the ground”.

and

Wyoming Office of Outdoor Recreation to construct a trailhead and 
initial trail. Construction began in May 2024 and the developed 
area is expected to be fully open to the public in 2025. In addition 
to the trailhead, this first phase of work will include 6.5 miles of 
non-motorized trail.

targeted update of the Belvoir Ranch Master Plan. The purpose 
of the Plan is to recognize existing conditions and provide 
implementation steps to achieve short-term goals at the Ranch.

The City of Cheyenne contracted with Wenk 
Associates and a team of consultants to complete 
the Belvoir Ranch Master Plan in 2008. The 2008

history and natural character of the Belvoir Ranch.

dollars. In addition to these improvements, the plan 
includes a robust discussion on existing conditions 
and Ranch history.
The most notable improvements that have 
taken place from the 2008 Master Plan is the 
development of the wind turbines on the Ranch.

Wind Energy
Wind Energy was cited as a potential use of the 
Belvoir Ranch in the 2008 Master Plan. The 2008 
Plan accounted for wind energy development along 
several ridges on the property. Wind energy was 
planned on the Ranch due to its close proximity to 
the Western Area Power Administration transmission 
line corridor and the high class of winds.
In 2019 to 2020 the wind turbine development 
potential of the Ranch came to fruition. NextEra 
energy developed 100+ wind turbines on the Ranch 
and the surrounding vicinity. Although the wind 
development on the Ranch was more expansive 
than initially proposed in the 2008 Plan, the use 
provides a steady stream of revenue from the lease 
agreement and has resulted in the development of 
several roads throughout the property.

Character Areas
Based on the feedback gathered from the public 
meetings and stakeholder groups the project 
team has determined that using a character areas 
approach to planning for the Ranch will ensure 
improvements will follow a predictable manner. The 
character areas are generally delineated on the 
map on the following page but do not need to follow 
distinct boundaries. The character areas approach 
provides a framework for determining goals for each 
character area and providing policies to achieve 
each of these goals. The goals and policies are 
described in this chapter with implementation 
steps for the policies in the final chapter. Although 
the character areas are not prescriptive, they do 
describe the uses that are acceptable within their 
general boundaries. If a use is not described within 
the character area section, it should go before the 
Belvoir Ranch Steering Committee to determine if it 
is an acceptable use on the property.
In general, the character areas of the Ranch are 
described by the following:
Natural and Recreation: This character area builds 
upon the current trail efforts and emphasizes the 
connection to the Colorado Open Spaces. Natural 
and Recreation is generally found on the west side 
of the property.
Energy and Services: This character area focuses 
on energy development, water resources, and the 
future landfill and is found throughout the Ranch.
History and Interpretation: This character area 
protects the cultural and military history of the 
Ranch with targeted efforts at interpretation. It is 
located at targeted areas on the Ranch.
Opportunity and Transition: The final character 
area is the Opportunity and Transition zone. This 
is where the Ranch presents opportunity for future 
development and transitions to the City, found 
primarily on the east side of the Belvoir.

The Character Areas approach to the management of the Belvoir Ranch will minimize conflicts between 
uses, set community expectations for development, and create a framework to consider new uses and 
update the plan moving forward. It is expected that this plan will be revisited every five years as conditions 
on the Ranch continue to evolve. This plan does not extensively explore all options, rather it provides 
steps individual departments or the City Council should take to enhance the management of the Belvoir. 
Furthermore, the Plan acknowledges actions already in progress, such as the development of the trails and 
wind turbines, and expands on how these developments should be considered in the future.
Conclusions
The Belvoir Ranch Master Plan Update acknowledges a variety of uses that could be considered at the 
Ranch. However, with innovation and expanding technologies, it should be noted that not all potential uses 
are cataloged in this document. In considering new uses at the Belvoir Ranch, the Belvoir Ranch Steering 
Committee should determine if the use is appropriate based on the following considerations: 
 » Does the use meet the defining characteristics of any of the Character Areas? If so, which Character 
Area should the use be located? 
 » Does the use conflict with any previously anticipated use on the Ranch? The use should not conflict 
with the prioritized uses of water resources, the landfill, or recreation. 
 » In what ways can the conflict between the proposed use and the priority uses be minimized? If 
mitigation is not possible or cost-prohibitive, the use is not appropriate for the Belvoir Ranch. 
 » What are the ongoing revenue and maintenance costs of the proposed use? If the ongoing costs 
outweigh the revenue, can these costs be privately funded? 
 » What is the duration of the proposed use? Will this impact the duration of other uses? 
 » If new land is acquired by the City of Cheyenne for the Ranch, the Steering Committee should assign 
the land a character area and designate appropriate uses.

The following pages show the implementation matrix for the action steps of each Character Area Goals. The 
implementation matrix explores the priority, cost, time frame, and responsible party for each action step. It 
should be noted that the action step may lead to further implementation based on the findings of a study 
that is completed or a policy that is created.
Priority: Priority is informed by feedback from the public and stakeholders. The Governing Body sets the final 
priority based in the information from the input process, their experience with their constituency, and their 
assessment of feasibility.
Cost: Cost is the estimated cost in both time working on the action step and cash investment. This 
estimated cost reflects the options available at the time of the writing of the master plan and significant 
swings could occur based on construction costs, new technologies, donations or other unanticipated 
changes. Lower-priority items may be implemented earlier due to cost considerations.
Duration: Duration is how far out the proposed action step is anticipated to be. Some action steps may be 
accelerated based on an unanticipated opportunities, or be extended due to unanticipated obstacles. 
 
Task: Task is the City agency anticipated to have stewardship over the action item. Some action items 
require collaboration between multiple agencies.
