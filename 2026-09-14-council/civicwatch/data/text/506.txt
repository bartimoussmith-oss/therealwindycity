WYOMING: 
 
Section 1.  The Governing Body of the City of Cheyenne finds and declares that: 
 
A. State and federal wastewater discharge permitting regulations require that publicly 
owned treatment works (POTWs) provide a written technical evaluation of the need to revise local 
limits following the issuance or reissuance of a National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System 
(NPDES) permit; 
 
B. On February 20, 2024, the State of Wyoming issued permits authorizing the 
discharge of treated wastewater from  the Dry Creek Water Reclamation Facility and the Crow 
Creek Water Reclamation Facility to receiving waters identified as Crow Creek, South Platte River 
Basin, in accordance with the requirements of the Wyoming Pollution Discharge Elimination 
System (WPDES) Permits WY0022934 and WY0022381, respectively; 
 
C. In compliance with the NPDES permitting requirements , the City of Cheyenne 
Board of Public Utilities (BOPU) submitted a proposed Sampling Plan for  review by the 
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) on January 22, 2025, and the EPA approved the 
Sampling Plan on June 2, 2025; 
 
D. In compliance with the NPDES permitting requirements , BOPU  submitted a 
proposed Pollutants of Concern (POCs) report for EPA review on  May 29, 2025 , providing 
information pertaining to the screening and evaluation of pollutants and  the proposed final 
determination of POCs to be included in the Local Limits Evaluation, and the EPA agreed with 
the POCs Evaluation on June 2, 2025; 
 
E. In compliance with the NPDES permitting requirements, BOPU  submitted a 
proposed technically based Local Limits Evaluation for EPA review on August 6, 2025, and the 
EPA deemed the proposed local limitations approvable on January 28, 2026; and 
 
F. To remain in compliance with the NPDES permitting requirements, to preserve the 
quality of Crow Creek, to maintain the plant at the highest performance levels, to protect the plant’s 
operating staff, and to protect the overall environment of the South Platte River Basin, the 
Governing Body determines  that it is necessary and appropriate to amend certain wastewater 
treatment and disposal provisions of the Cheyenne Municipal Code as provided in this Ordinance.

13.20.050 - General prohibitions. 
 
A. No industrial user shall contribute or cause to be contributed, directly or indirectly, to the 
POTW any pollutant or wastewater which will pass through or cause interference with the 
operation or performance of the POTW. These general prohibitions and the specific 
prohibitions in subsection B of this section shall apply to all the industrial users of the 
POTW, whether or not they are subject to pretreatment standards or requirements. 
 
B. Specific Prohibitions. No industrial user shall contribute the following substances to the 
POTW: 
 
1. Any substances which by reason of their nature or quantity will create a fire or 
explosion hazard or be injurious to the POTW or to the operation of the POTW, 
including, but not limited to, waste streams with a closed cup flashpoint of less than 
one hundred forty (140) degrees Fahrenheit or sixty (60) degrees Celsius using the test 
methods in 40 CFR Section 261.21; 
 
2. Any solid or viscous substances which will cause obstruction to sewer flow or other 
interference with the operation of the POTW such as, but not limited to, fats, oils and 
grease, garbage with particles greater than one -half inch in any dimension, or any 
material which can be disposed of as trash; 
 
3. Any biological, chemical, or enzyme product, used on its own or in conjunction with 
other substances, that causes the liquefaction or emulsification or the act of liquifying 
or emulsifying fats, oil or grease; 
 
4. Petroleum oil, nonbiodegradable cutting oil, or products of mineral oil origin in 
amounts that will cause pass through or interference; 
 
5. Any wastewater having a pH less than five or having any other corrosive property 
capable of causing damage or hazard to structures, equipment or personnel of the 
POTW; 
 
6. Any substance which may cause a public nuisance, cause hazard to life or prevent entry 
into the sewers for maintenance and repair; 
 
7. Heated substances in amounts which will prevent entry into the sewer system of 
authorized personnel or will inhibit biological activity in the POTW resulting in 
interference, but in no case heat in such quantities that the temperature at the POTW 
treatment plant exceeds one hundred four (104) degrees Fahrenheit or forty (40) 
degrees Celsius. A limit may be required in particular situations where the temperature 
has caused or has potential to cause pass through of an interceptor, pass through or 
interference in the POTW;

POTW in a quantity that may cause acute worker health and safety problems; 
 
9. Any trucked or hauled pollutants, except at discharge points designated by the BOPU; 
 
10. Any pollutant, including oxygen demanding pollutants, released in a discharge at a flow

foaming in the POTW; 
 
17. Bulk, expired, outdated, or concentrated prescription and non -prescription drugs, that

C. No chemicals, or substances, including, but not limited to, paints, solvents, boiler or water

substances, or wastes that leak or have potential to leak or discharge into the POTW which 
may create an explosion hazard or in any way have a deleter ious effect to the POTW or 
constitute a nuisance or a hazard to POTW personnel, the general public, the environment, 
or the receiving stream shall be prohibited.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
The City of Cheyenne Board of Public Utilities (BOPU) is authorized by the State of Wyoming 
to discharge from the Dry Creek Water Reclamation Facility and Crow Creek Water 
Reclamation Facility to receiving waters named Crow Creek, South Platte River Basin in 
accordance with the requirements of Wyoming Pollution Discharge Elimination System 
(WPDES) Permits WY0022934 and WY0022381 respectively. The issuance date for both 
permits is February 20, 2024. This Local Limits Evaluation is for both treatment facilities. 
The General Pretreatment Regulations per 40 CFR Part 403 require that every POTW develop 
and implement local limits to protect their treatment plant, sewage system, biosolids and 
receiving water from adverse impacts from industrial and commercial wastewater dischargers.  
Each POTW with an approved pretreatment program shall continue to develop these limits as 
necessary and effectively enforce such limits. EPA regulations require that POTWs must 
provide a written technical evaluation of the need to revise local limits following NPDES permit 
issuance or reissuance. This technical evaluation has been conducted in accordance with 
EPA’s "Local Limits Development Guidance" July 2004 and the USEPA - Region 8 
“Technically-Based Local Limits Development Strategy” April 2003 including the associated 
appendices and the Region 8 Local Limits Spreadsheet. 
All records of the monitoring data used in the development of these technically-based local 
limits are on-file and available for review at the Dry Creek Water Reclamation Facility. These 
records will be maintained on-file as long as the current local limits are in effect. 
The development of this technically based local limits evaluation includes the following steps: 
•  Preliminary data collection 
- Plant characteristics including compliance history 
- Influent flows (domestic, non-domestic, SIUs, hauled wastes) 
- Sampling data (plant influent/effluent scans, domestic and commercial sampling, 
SIUs, and Biosolids)  
•  Compilation of information 
- WYDES Permit limitations 
- Water Quality Standards 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
9 | Page 
 
      
 
- Sludge Quality Standards 
•  Preliminary identification of Pollutants of Concern (POC) from the preliminary data 
and compilation of information 
•  Calculation of plant removal efficiencies 
•  Calculation of Allowable Headworks Loading (AHL), Maximum Allowable Headworks 
Loadings (MAHL), and Maximum Allowable Industrial Loading (MAIL) 
•  Calculation of Uniform Local Limits 
•  Assessment of calculated Local Limits 
 
In preparation for this technically-based study, the BOPU submitted a proposed Sampling Plan 
for EPA review on January 22, 2025. The plan, reviewed and approved on March 3, 2025, 
contained information regarding sampling practices conducted by BOPU and identified the 
monitoring data and information used in the preparation of the local limits evaluation. The 
BOPU also submitted a proposed Pollutants of Concern Evaluation for EPA Review on May 
29, 2025; this submittal provided information pertaining to the screening and evaluation of 
pollutants and proposed final determination of POCs to be included in the Local Limits 
evaluation. The EPA reviewed and deemed the Pollutants of Concern Evaluation approvable 
as a component of the Local Limits Development Evaluation on June 2, 2025.  
 
Based on EPA guidance, the following proposed local discharge limitations have been 
developed based on the Maximum Allowable Headworks Loading (MAHL), calculated in 
pounds per day, for each pollutant of concern. MAHLs estimate the maximum combined 
loadings that can be received at the POTW’s headworks from all sources. A pollutants MAHL 
is determined by first calculating its Allowable Headworks Loading (AHL) for each 
environmental criterion; the most stringent AHL constitutes the MAHL. A safety factor is 
applied to protect the POTW from uncertainties in data. Region 8 requires a safety + growth 
factor of at least ten (10) percent. After the MAHL, adjusted with a safety factor, has been 
determined for each POC, the portion of the pollutant loading from non-controllable domestic 
sources and pollutant loading from commercial sources not operating under a discharge permit 
is subtracted leaving the Maximum Allowable Loading (MAL) available to Significant Industrial 
Users (SIUs). An additional growth allowance is applied to protect the POTW against growth 
and expansion of existing SIUs.  The calculated MAL less the growth factor determines the 
Maximum Allowable Industrial Loading (MAIL). Reserve loading is subtracted from the MAIL to 
allow for loading from future SIUs. The MAIL less reserve loading is then pollutant loadings 
allocated to SIUs. The BOPU will allocate the loadings to the SIUs through uniform 
concentration-based discharge limitations.   
 
The USEPA considers the adoption of, or revisions to the MAIL a Substantial Program 
Modification. As such, the Approval Authority (EPA Region 8 Pretreatment Administration) 
must issue a public notice of the POTWs request for approval and must provide an opportunity

 (3) Assures the changes will comply with state laws and established standards and

1.0 INTRODUCTION 
The City of Cheyenne governing body, operating under the Mayor/Council form of government, 
reviews and approves the Sewer Enterprise Fund as recommended by the BOPU. The BOPU 
has exclusive control of all sewer utility systems owned by the City of Cheyenne. The BOPU 
provides water and sewer services for the City of Cheyenne, Warren Air Force Base, and 
South Cheyenne Water and Sanitation District. Approximately 23,000 residential, 1630 
commercial, and 20 industrial sewer taps have been authorized by the BOPU.  
Treated wastewater produced at the Dry Creek and Crow Creek Water Reclamation Facilities 
discharge to receiving waters named Crow Creek, which is part of the South Platte River 
Basin. Wyoming Pollution Discharge Elimination System (WPDES) Permits WY0022934 and 
WY0022381 issued to the Dry Creek and Crow Creek treatment facilities respectively, 
establish end-of-pipe effluent limits that are protective of all receiving water designated uses as 
defined in the Wyoming Water Quality Rules and Regulations. Designated uses applicable to 
the receiving water include warm water aquatic life and fish consumption. Wyoming Water 
Quality Rules and Regulations, Chapter 1 establish the State Water Quality Standards for the 
protection of aquatic life and human health. 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
12 | Page 
 
      
 
Sewer sludge produced at the Crow Creek facility is sent to the Dry Creek facility for further 
processing. The produced Biosolids are recycled as a fertilizer and soil amendment and land 
applied per EPA Biosolids requirements provided in 40 CFR Part 503.  
 
The purpose and goals of an Industrial Pretreatment Program are: 
•  To prevent the introduction of pollutants into the Publicly-Owned Treatment Works 
(POTW) that will interfere with the operation of the system or contaminate the resulting 
sludge; 
•  To prevent the introduction of pollutants into the POTW which will pass through the 
system, inadequately treated, into receiving waters or the atmosphere or otherwise be 
incompatible with the system; 
•  To improve the opportunity to recycle and reclaim wastewaters and sludges from the 
system; 
•  To provide for equitable distribution among users of the cost of the POTW; 
•  To provide for and promote the general health, safety and welfare of the citizens 
residing within the City and connecting jurisdictions; 
•  To enable the City to comply with its WYDES discharge permit conditions, sludge use 
and disposal requirements, and any other Federal or State laws to which the POTW is 
subject; and 
•  To prevent adverse impacts to worker health and safety due to the discharge of 
pollutants from industrial users. 
 
These goals are accomplished through administering: 
•  National Categorical Standards (industry-specific effluent limits) 
•  Prohibited Discharge Standards (general and specific prohibitions) 
•  Enforceable Technically Based Local Limits (TBLLs) 
 
The development and adoption of TBLLs, based on site- specific conditions, protects the 
treatment plant, collection system, biosolids, and receiving stream from adverse impacts from 
industrial and commercial dischargers.  
The development of the proposed TBLLs have been based on site conditions, sampling data 
collected over the previous five years, current regulations pertaining to water quality and 
biosolids standards, and the best professional judgement of BOPU.

2.3 Dry Creek Water Reclamation Facility Process Descriptions 
Wastewater from residential, commercial, and industrial sources is conveyed to the Dry 
Creek Water Reclamation Facility (DC WRF) via the Crow Creek and Dry Creek 
interceptors. The DC WRF site is located approximately one-half mile southwest of the 
Campstool Road interchange with Interstate 80 (8911 Campstool Road). The plant 
discharges into Crow Creek and is at an elevation of approximately 5,900 feet above 
mean sea level. 
 
Liquid Stream Wastewater Treatment and Handling: 
Raw wastewater from the Crow Creek and Dry Creek interceptors is directed to the wet 
well of Dry Creek’s Influent Pumping Station (IPS). The IPS pumps the raw wastewater 
to the Headworks Building for preliminary treatment. There, the flow is divided into two 
parallel flow channels that pass through fine stair screens (1/4-inch opening) designed 
to remove rags and debris. The stair screens also include a washing/compacting 
system. The washed and compacted screenings are pressed into a pipe which 
discharges into a dumpster located in the North Side of the Headworks Building. 
 
Following screening, the wastewater is conveyed to a vortex grit separation (Pista Grit) 
basin. The grit basin is located inside the Headworks Building. The grit slurry is pumped 
from the bottom of the grit basin to a classifier/dewatering unit located in the truck-
loading bay. The dewatered grit is deposited in a second dumpster in the North Side of 

Local Limits Evaluation  
 
15 | Page 
 
      
 
the Headworks Building. The dumpsters are emptied daily and taken to landfills. 
Following the grit-removal process, the wastewater flows through the main Parshall 
flume located on the west side of the Maintenance Building. The flume is fitted with an 
ultrasonic level sensor for flow measurement. The level/flow signal from the Parshall 
flume is transmitted to the supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) system 
and is logged as the DC WRF’s influent flow. The flow is split between the North 
Primary Complex and the South Primary Complex.  
 
The North Primary Complex flows are directed to the North Primary flow splitter, where 
the flow is spilt evenly to the two North Complex primary clarifiers. Flow to the South 
Primary Complex is spilt evenly to the two South Complex primary clarifiers. The North 
and South primary clarifiers provide for removal of settleable solids (primary sludge). 
The settled sludge is removed from the clarifiers via pumps located in the Primary 
Sludge Pumping Stations. Floating fats, oils, and greases are also removed from the 
wastewater at the primary clarifiers to a solids pit that is pumped out weekly. Effluent 
from the north and south primary clarifiers recombine at the Primary Effluent Flow 
Splitter (PEFS) and then spill over a set of weir troughs to discharge boxes on the north 
and south sides of the structure.  
 
Wastewater spilling into the north discharge box is directed to Anoxic Basin Train 1 
(north). Similarly, wastewater spilling into the south discharge box is directed to Anoxic 
Basin Train 2 (south). Return activated sludge (RAS) from the Secondary Sludge 
Pumping Station is also directed to the center of the PEFS prior to conveyance to the 
anoxic basins. The weirs divide primary effluent and RAS flows proportionally between 
the two anoxic basin trains based on the volume of each train. Each anoxic basin train 
is divided into three reactors (in series) by submerged baffle walls. Each reactor is 
provided with a floating mixer to keep solids in suspension as the liquid flows through 
the train. The anoxic basins are not aerated. Anoxic Basin Train 1 discharges to 
Integrated Fixed-Film Activated Sludge (IFAS) Basin 1, and Anoxic Basin Train 2 
discharges to IFAS Basin 2.  
 
The anoxic (unaerated) process trains provide for the biological treatment of nitrates & 
nitrites, as well as some BOD treatment. The biomass (microorganisms) performing the 
treatment are supplied by the RAS and circulate freely within the wastewater flow to 
form mixed liquor. Each IFAS basin is divided into two reactors in series. The IFAS 
process uses high-density polyethylene (HDPE) plastic media suspended in the basins 
as biofilm carrier elements. The IFAS process trains provide for the biological treatment 
of BOD and ammonia. The biomass (microorganisms) performing the treatment are 
supported by the plastic media, as well as circulating freely within mixed liquor. The 
discharge side of each IFAS reactor is provided with submerged cylindrical stainless 
steel sieve screens that allow the mixed liquor to exit the reactor but retain the media. 
The floor of each IFAS reactor is provided with a coarse medium-bubble stainless steel 
aeration grid. The aeration grid provides oxygen to the biomass, as well as mixing 
energy for the reactor. 
 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
16 | Page 
 
      
 
The effluent structure at the effluent end of each IFAS basin is provided with two 
submersible type recycle pumps. The recycle pumps direct a portion of the IFAS 
effluent flow back to the head of the anoxic basins. Each IFAS basin’s effluent structure 
is provided with a fixed effluent control weir such that any IFAS effluent not recycled 
back to the anoxic trains, spills over the weirs and is directed to the Secondary 
Clarifiers. The Blower Building is located south of IFAS Basin 2. Five centrifugal blowers 
located in the Blower Building provide process air to the IFAS basins. Dissolved oxygen 
(DO) probes, oxidation reduction potential (ORP) probes located in the IFAS basins, 
provide process control data to the SCADA system. The SCADA system uses the 
process control signal from the DO probes to automatically adjust the airflow delivered 
to each IFAS basin.  
 
The non-recycled portion of the IFAS effluent flows by gravity to the Secondary Clarifier 
Influent Splitter Box where it is proportionally distributed to Secondary Clarifiers No. 1, 
No. 2, and No. 3. The biological solids (secondary sludge) settle out from the treated 
wastewater in the secondary clarifiers. The base of each clarifier is provided with a 
hopper for collection of the settled sludge. The settled sludge is removed from the 
clarifiers via pumps located in the Secondary Sludge Pumping Station. Most of the 
secondary sludge is directed to the PEFS as RAS. A portion of the secondary sludge is 
directed to the sludge thickening facility as waste activated sludge (WAS). Floating 
scum is also removed at the secondary clarifiers via rotating skimmer arms. Clarifier 
overflow (secondary effluent) is piped to the Ultraviolet (UV) Disinfection Facility.  
 
The UV Disinfection Facility consists of two parallel open channels fitted with UV-
generating modules. The UV modules, cleaning tank, jib crane, and electrical support 
equipment are housed inside the UV Building. A common effluent channel fitted with 
weir troughs is provided downstream of the UV treatment channels. The disinfected final 
effluent exits the effluent chamber by spilling over the weir troughs and entering the 
non-potable water (NPW) Pumping Station wetwell. Any effluent not pumped out by the 
NPW pumps spills over a control weir and enters the final effluent pipe. The final 
effluent pipe is fitted with a flowmeter for flow measurement prior to exiting the plant site 
and discharging to Crow Creek. The flow signal from the effluent flowmeter is 
transmitted to the SCADA system and is logged as effluent flow. 
 
 Solids Stream Treatment and Handling: 
The solids stream consists of the organic and inorganic solids (sludge) removed from 
the wastewater in the primary and secondary clarifiers. The sludge is pumped from the 
bottom of the clarifiers and stabilized in the anaerobic digesters. All of the BOPU’s 
sludge stabilization and processing facilities are located at the DC WRF. Therefore, all 
of the solids collected at the Crow Creek Water Reclamation Facility (CC WRF) are 
directed to the DC WRF via the Crow Creek Interceptor.  
 
When the CC WRF solids reach the DC WRF, they are settled out in the DC WRF 
primary clarifiers. Floating scum (grease, oil, fat. etc.) that has been removed from the 
surface of the primary and secondary clarifiers is not normally directed to the digesters. 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
17 | Page 
 
      
 
Primary scum is normally stabilized with fly ash in the drying beds. Secondary scum is 
normally directed back to the head of the plant. Primary sludge is pumped directly into 
the Primary Digester for stabilization. Secondary sludge (WAS) must first be thickened 
before it is fed to the Primary Digester. WAS is pumped to the Sludge Thickening 
Building where it is thickened (5 percent solids) by one of the two the rotary drum 
thickeners. The Sludge Thickening Building houses two rotary drum thickeners, a 
polymer feed system, thickened sludge holding tanks, thickened sludge pumps, and 
electrical support equipment. Polymer is added to the sludge ahead of the drum 
thickeners to enhance thickening performance. The thickened WAS is pumped from the 
thickened sludge holding tanks to the Primary Digester by the thickened sludge pumps.  
 
The liquid separated from the WAS in the thickening process is directed to the head of 
the plant for treatment. The Primary and Secondary Digesters at the DC WRF stabilize 
the sludge prior to dewatering, air drying, and final land application. The sludge in the 
digesters is heated via a heat exchanger. The combination of heat and detention time 
provides the degree of stabilization needed to meet the regulations. The two tanks are 
connected with a gravity transfer line. The liquid level in the Primary Digester is held at 
a constant elevation by a fixed position effluent pipe connected to the gravity transfer 
line. As the liquid level in the Primary Digester rises, flow is transferred to the 
Secondary Digester by gravity by the fixed position effluent pipe.  
 
The digested sludge is pumped from the Secondary Digester to the Sludge Dewatering 
Building. The Secondary Digester provides additional detention time, as well as acting 
as a flow equalization tank so that flows to the Sludge Dewatering Building do not have 
to exactly match flows into the digester. The solids in the digester are kept in 
suspension with gas-mixing guns distributed inside the digesters, Methane gas is 
generated during the digestion process and is collected for use in powering the heat 
exchanger. Excess gas is burned at the digester’s flare. Dewatered sludge pumps, 
located in the basement of the Control Building, convey the digested sludge to the 
centrifuges located in the Sludge Dewatering Building where it is dewatered by one of 
the two the high solids centrifuges. 
 
The Sludge Dewatering Building houses two high solids centrifuges, a polymer feed 
system, a dewatered sludge truck-loading bay, and electrical and mechanical support 
equipment. Polymer is added to the sludge ahead of the centrifuges to enhance 
dewatering performance. The dewatered digested sludge is deposited on a screw 
conveyor which moves the sludge into the truck loading bay. The dewatered sludge is 
then trucked to the drying beds for supplemental drying prior to being hauled off-site 
and land applied. The liquid separated from the sludge in the dewatering process is 
called centrate. The centrate is conveyed by gravity to the Centrate Handling Basin. 
Centrate has a high concentration of ammonia, so it should either receive some 
treatment prior to entering the main flow stream or it should be metered into the flow 
stream during periods of low influent ammonia concentration. The Centrate Handling 
Basin can serve as a pretreatment tank or as a fill and dump tank.

2.6 Crow Creek Water Reclamation Facility Process Descriptions 
Wastewater from residential, commercial, and industrial sources is conveyed to the CC 
WRF via the Crow Creek Interceptor and the Lexington Line. The CC WRF site is 
located approximately one-half mile southeast of the College Avenue interchange with 
Interstate 80 (4403 Livingston Avenue). The plant discharges into Crow Creek and is at 
an elevation of approximately 6,000 feet above mean sea level.  
 
 
Liquid Stream Wastewater Treatment and Handling 
 
Raw wastewater from the Crow Creek Interceptor and Lexington Line is directed to the 
wetwell of Crow Creek’s IPS. The IPS is located upstream of the screening and 
degritting processes across from the Headworks and Control Buildings. The pumps are 
designed to handle raw wastewater, including rags and solids up to 6 inches in 
diameter. IPS pumps the raw wastewater to the Headworks Building for preliminary 
treatment. There, the flow passes through a fine screen (1/4-inch opening) designed to 
remove rags and debris. The Headworks Building contains a single flow channel in 
which a Parkson Bioguard screening/washing system is installed.

The Parkson Bioguard system consists of a fine screen attached to a screenings 
washing/compacting/dewatering system. The washed and compacted screenings are 
deposited onto a belt conveyor which discharges into a dumpster located in the 
attached garage. Following screening, the wastewater exists the Headworks Building 
superstructure and enters a grit separation basin. The grit basin is attached to the south 
side of the Headworks Building but is otherwise open to the atmosphere. The grit slurry 
is pumped from the bottom of the grit basin back inside the Headworks Building to a 
classifier/dewatering unit. The dewatered grit is deposited on the same conveyor as the 
washed screenings and conveyed to the same dumpster. The dumpster is emptied 
weekly and taken to landfill.  
 
Following the grit-removal process, the wastewater flows through a Parshall flume 
located on the east side of the Headworks Building. The flow signal from the Parshall 
flume is transmitted to the SCADA system and is logged as the CC WRF’s influent flow. 
From the Parshall flume, the screened and degritted wastewater flows via an open 
channel to the primary influent distribution manhole where the flow is spilt evenly to the 
two primary clarifiers. Effluent from the two primary clarifiers recombines and flows by 
gravity to the PEFS structure through a 30-inch pipe. 
 
Primary effluent enters the PEFS from the north in the center of the structure and then 
spills over weirs on the east and west side of the structure. Wastewater spilling over the 
east weir is directed to the east moving bed biofilm reactor (MBBR) train via a dedicated 
30-inch pipe. Similarly, wastewater spilling over the west weir is directed to the west 
MBBR train via its own 30-inch pipe. The weirs divide flow equally between the east and 
west MBBR trains.  
 
Two parallel oxic MBBR (aerated) process trains provide for biological treatment of the 
primary effluent prior to discharge to the secondary clarifiers. Each process train 
consists of four oxic MBBR reactors in series. The MBBR process uses high-density 
polyethylene (HDPE) plastic media suspended in the basins as biofilm carrier elements. 
Cylindrical stainless steel submerged sieves retain the media within each reactor. The 
first two reactors in each train are designated for BOD removal, and the last two 
reactors in each train are designated for nitrification (ammonia removal). The north 
exterior wall of the basin and the influent structure and piping are designed to 
accommodate addition of the pre-denitrification Anoxic MBBR basins in the future.  
 
The effluent structure at the effluent end of the MBBR Complex is sized and configured 
to provide for the future addition of recycle pumps as required for pre-denitrification. The 
effluent structure consists of a fixed effluent control weir and effluent chamber. The 
Blower Building is located immediately south of the MBBR Complex. Four centrifugal 
blowers located in the Blower Building provide process air to each oxic MBBR reactor. 
Dissolved oxygen (DO) probes and oxidation reduction potential (ORP) probes located 
in the MBBR basins, provide process control data to the SCADA system. The SCADA 
system uses the process control signal from the DO probes to automatically adjust the 
airflow delivered to each MBBR train. The MBBR effluent flows by gravity to the 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
21 | Page 
 
      
 
Secondary Clarifier Influent Distribution Manhole where it is evenly distributed to 
Secondary Clarifiers No. 1 and No. 2. The biological solids sloughed from the media in 
the MBBR reactors settle out from the treated wastewater in the secondary clarifiers. 
Clarifier overflow (secondary effluent) is conveyed to the UV Disinfection Facility via a 
combination of flumes and piping for disinfection.  
 
The UV Disinfection Facility consists of a single open channel fitted with UV-generating 
modules. The UV modules, cleaning tank, lifting hoist, and electrical support equipment 
are housed inside the UV Building. An effluent chamber fitted with control weirs is 
provided at the effluent end of the UV Building. The disinfected final effluent exits the 
effluent chamber by spilling over the control weirs and flowing into a 30-inch final 
effluent pipe. The final effluent pipe is fitted with a flowmeter and discharges to Crow 
Creek. The flow signal from the 30-inch flowmeter is transmitted to the SCADA system 
and is logged as the CC WRF’s effluent flow. Two 24-inch pipes, located upstream of 
the control weirs, connect the UV effluent chamber to the wet pit for the Recycled Water 
Transfer Pumps. The wet pit and pumps are located in the Secondary Disinfection and 
Pumping Building. Since the transfer pump wet pit and UV Treatment Facility are 
hydraulically connected, they will operate at the same water surface elevation. Any 
disinfected UV effluent not pumped out by the reuse feed pumps spills over the effluent 
chamber control weirs and is discharged to Crow Creek. The SCADA system uses the 
effluent flow signal and level sensors in the effluent chamber to prevent the feed pumps 
from dropping the water surface at the UV Modules below the control weir level.  
 
Solids Stream Treatment and Handling 
The solids stream consists of the organic and inorganic solids (sludge) removed from 
the wastewater in the primary and secondary clarifiers. The settled solids are pumped 
from the bottom of the clarifiers and combined with the floating scum (grease, oil, fat. 
etc.) that has been removed from the surface of the clarifiers. All of the BOPU’s sludge 
stabilization and processing facilities are located at the DC WRF. Therefore, all of the 
solids collected at the CC WRF are pumped into the IPS overflow line which connects to 
the interceptor feeding the DC WRF. A small bypass line is provided in the IPS 
discharge piping that provides a flushing flow of raw wastewater into the overflow line 
keep the sludge/solid in suspension until they reach the DC WRF. When the CC WRF 
solids reach the DC WRF, they are settled out in the primary clarifiers at that site. The 
sludge drying beds at the CC WRF are currently used for stabilization of grease 
collected through the BOPU’s oil and grease mitigation program.

6.0 POLLUTANTS OF CONCERN 
The BOPU submitted a proposed Pollutants of Concern Evaluation for EPA Review on 
May 29, 2025. This submittal provided information pertaining to the screening and 
evaluation of pollutants and proposed final determination of POCs to be included in the 
Local Limits evaluation. The EPA reviewed and agreed with the Pollutants of Concern 
Evaluation on June 2, 2025.  
 
Pollutants of Concern (POC) are pollutants that must be carried through the local limits 
evaluation. A POC is any pollutant that might reasonably be expected to be discharged 
to the POTW in sufficient amounts to cause pass through or interference, cause 
problems in its collection system, cause operational problems, or jeopardize its 
workers. 
 
EPA Local Limits Development Guidance identifies POCs as any pollutants falling into 
the following categories: 
 
1. National POCs - Arsenic, Cadmium, Copper, Chromium, Cyanide, Lead, 
Mercury, Molybdenum, Nickel, Selenium, Silver, Zinc, 5-day Biochemical Oxygen 
Demand, Total Suspended Solids, and Ammonia; 
2. Any pollutant listed in your State Water Quality Standards for your receiving 
water or are listed in your NPDES permit; 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
31 | Page 
 
      
 
3. Any pollutant specified in 40 CFR 122 Appendix D, Tables II or V that was 
detected in an influent or effluent sample and its concentration was equal to or 
greater than 0.05 mg/l; 
4. Any pollutant that is present at 0.01 mg/l or greater (above the method detection 
limit if MDL is >0.01 mg/l) and has a Bioconcentration Factor (BCF) of 300 or 
greater;  
5. Any other pollutant not covered by category 3 or 4, that is determined to be 
present at equal to, or greater than, 0.1 mg/l in the influent or effluent samples 
and that may be toxic to the POTW, receiving waters, worker health or biosolids 
if discharged; 
6. Any pollutant with a current average influent (headworks) loading greater than 
the POTW design loading; 
7. Any pollutant identified through WET characterization studies as being present 
and suspected or responsible for toxicity; 
8. Any pollutant identified in biosolids analytical results which is not a pollutant that 
would be expected or is at a concentration that would be considered atypical; 
9. Any pollutant that has the potential to interfere with POTW operations or are 
known to cause operational or maintenance problems; 
10. Any other pollutant specifically designated by the POTW Pretreatment Program 
and/or Approval Authority as a pollutant of concern. 
 
EPA recommends that any pollutant that has a “reasonable potential” to be discharged 
in amounts that could exceed water quality standards or other environmental criteria 
should be considered a POC and evaluated accordingly.

7.0 MINIMUM DETECTION LEVELS AND BELOW DETECTION LEVELS  
Environmental data sets frequently contain values that are below analytical detection 
methods. In the TBLL evaluation, the BOPU is following the EPA conservative position 
that a pollutant is present in some concentration even if the analysis provides a result 
below the reporting limit (non-detection). During the calculations of influent and effluent 
average concentrations used in maximum allowable loadings computations, the BOPU 
used a conservative approach of substituting ½ the reporting limit for non-detections.  
During the Pollutants of Concern evaluation, the pollutants warranting Reasonable 
Potential statistical analysis, the Robust Regression on Order Statistics (ROS) method 
to “censor” the non-detection results was used. The ROS method plots the detected 
values on a probability plot then calculates a linear regression line in order to 
approximate the parameters of the underlying (assumed) distribution. This fitted 
distribution is then utilized to generate imputed estimates for each of the censored 
measurements, which are then combined with the known (detected) values to the 
summary statistics of interest.

8.2 Metals Translator

total analysis, the POTW will evaluate the pollutant as total recoverable. Established 
conversion factors will be used when environmental criteria are expressed as limitations 
in the dissolved or partially dissolved form to convert the criteria to total recoverable in 
the development of a local limit.

9.0 REMOVAL EFFICIENCY CALCULATIONS 
Removal efficiency is the fraction or percentage of the influent pollutant loading that is 
removed from the waste stream across an entire wastewater treatment works or 
specific processes. Per EPA guidance, there are three removal efficiency calculation 
methodologies commonly used by POTWs: the Average Daily Removal Efficiency 
(ADRE) method, the Mean Removal Efficiency (MRE) method, and the Decile method.  
Each of these methods uses a data set consisting of influent and lagged effluent POTW 
sampling results.   
 
Removal efficiency methodologies vary by degree of data quality and calculation 
method. A review of the data quality has been performed. Per EPA guidance, 
influent/effluent paired sampling events in which the influent level was below the 
detection limit were removed from the data set before calculating removal efficiencies. 
Because plant influent flows are relatively consistent, the BOPU calculated the removal 
efficiencies based on pollutant concentrations versus influent/effluent pollutant 
loadings. Data used in the removal efficiency calculations was obtained from 
influent/effluent pollutant scans (January 1, 2020 through December 31, 2024). A 
summary of the calculated removal efficiencies for the three methods is provided in 
Table 15. 
9.1 ADRE Method 
The ADRE is calculated using matched pairs of influent/effluent sampling results. The 
removal efficiency percentage for each matched pair is calculated using the following 
formula: 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
39 | Page 
 
      
 
 
Daily Removal Efficiency (DRE) = [Influent Concentration – Effluent Concentration] / 
Influent Concentration x 100 
 
The individual daily removal results are then averaged to determine the final removal 
percentage (ADRE). 
 
For the Dry Creek facility, removal efficiencies using the ADRE method were not 
calculated for Cadmium, Chromium, Lead, Mercury, and Silver due to the limited 
number of matched pairs of influent/effluent sampling results above the reporting limit. 
 
For the Crow Creek facility, removal efficiencies using the ADRE method not calculated 
for Cadmium, Chromium, Lead, and Silver due to the limited number of matched pairs 
of influent/effluent sampling results above the reporting limit. 
9.2 MRE Method 
The MRE is calculated by using the same general formula as for the ADRE, but instead 
of averaging the individual matched pair daily removal percentages, the entire set of 
influent values is first averaged to determine the average influent value and the same 
is done for the entire set of effluent values. These average values are then used in the 
removal efficiency formula shown above. The MRE can be based on influent and 
effluent sample values that are not always paired. If the influent level was below the 
detection limit, the associated effluent result was removed from the data set. Censored 
data (1/2 RL) was substituted for effluent results reported as below the detection limit 
threshold when there was an associated influent result at or above the detection limit.  
 
For the Dry Creek facility, the removal efficiency using the MRE method was not 
calculated for Silver due to the limited number of influent and effluent sampling results 
above the reporting limit. 
 
For the Crow Creek facility, the removal efficiency using the MRE method was not 
calculated for Cadmium, and Silver due to the limited number of influent or effluent 
sampling results above the reporting limit. 
9.3 Decile Method 
The Decile Method, unlike the ADRE and MRE methods, considers how often the 
actual plant daily removal efficiency will be above or below a specified removal rate, 
thereby taking into account the variability of POTW removal efficiencies over time.  
Each decile is the value below which a certain percentage of the DREs fall. For 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
40 | Page 
 
      
 
example, the first decile is the value below which 10% of the DREs fall. Similarly, the 
second decile is the value below which 20% of the DREs fall, on up to the ninth decile, 
which is the value below which 90% of the DREs fall. The fifth decile is the median and 
half of the DREs fall below this number. The main value of the decile approach is that it 
provides an estimate of how often a POTW is expected to exceed certain removal 
values, such as the ADRE and MRE. For the example, assuming the MRE for a 
particular pollutant is 35%; if the MRE falls in the third decile (25%ile), the plant’s actual 
removal rate will exceed the MRE 75% of the time.  If an Allowable Headworks Loading 
for that particular pollutant is based on sludge land application criteria and used the 
35% MRE and the plant has an actual removal efficiency of 70%, more of the pollutant 
may end up in the sludge than allowed for, thus resulting in a potential exceedance. To 
apply the decile approach, a minimum of nine “matched pairs” of DREs are required. 
With this limitation, the BOPU was able to calculate Decile Removal Efficiencies for 
Arsenic, Copper, Molybdenum and Zinc for both facilities.

EPA guidance recommends that POTWs base their Technically-Based Local Limits 
(TBLLs) on Maximum Allowable Headworks Loading (MAHL) calculated for each 
pollutant of concern. A MAHL is the upper limit of pollutant loading at which a POTW 
will not violate any treatment plant or environmental criteria developed to prevent 
process inhibition, interference, effluent limitation, or sludge quality standards. MAHLs 
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
44 | Page 
 
      
 
are the basis for local limits. A pollutant’s MAHL is determined by first calculating the 
Allowable Headworks Loading (AHL) for each limiting criterion for each pollutant; the 
most stringent AHL would be the MAHL. The AHL for each pollutant is a function of 
limiting criteria (environmental or process), plant flows, and plant removal efficiency.

13.0 MAXIMUM ALLOWABLE INDUSTRIAL LOADING (MAIL)  
The MAHLs provided in Table 21 estimate the maximum combined loadings that can 
be received (domestic, commercial, and industrial) at the POTW’s headworks from all 
sources and represent the upper limit of influent pollutant loadings at which a POTW 
will not violate any treatment plant or environmental criteria developed to prevent 
process inhibition or interference, or violation of effluent, or sludge quality standards. 
 
The Maximum Allowable Industrial Loading (MAIL) developed for each pollutant of 
concern represents the amount of the calculated MAHLs the POTW is able to allocate 
to Industrial permitted users. The available loadings are typically allocated through 
concentration-based discharge limitations. Consistent with the City’s Municipal Codes, 
the limitations developed by the BOPU are enforced upon Significant Industrial Users 
(SIUs) or Industrial Users issued a discharge permit.  
 
The equation for calculating the MAIL for each pollutant is as follows: 
 
MAL = (MAHL Less Safety Factor – LUNC) 
MAIL = (MAL Less GA)   
 
MAL = Maximum Allowable Loading  
Local Limits Evaluation  
 
52 | Page 
 
      
 
MAIL = Maximum Allowable Industrial Loading in lbs/Day 
SF = Safety Factor (percent) 
LUNC = Loading from uncontrolled sources (domestic + commercial) in lbs/Day 
calculated as 8.34 * residential/commercial concentration in mg/L* Average 
resident/commercial influent flow in MGD 
GA = Growth and Expansion Allowance in lbs/day, (portion of the loading held in 
reserve to protect the POTW against expansion of current permitted industries) 
 
The first step in calculating the final MAIL is to calculate the Maximum Allowable 
Loadings (MAL). The MAL is calculated by applying a safety factor to the Maximum 
Allowable Headworks Loadings. After the safety factor has been applied, loadings from 
residential/commercial sources are subtracted. The final step in calculating the MAIL is 
applying a Growth and Expansion factor to the MAL.

limits such as variability and quality of the POTW’s data, potential for industrial user slug 
loadings (e.g., as a result of a chemical spill), and potential general service area growth.

13.4 Growth Allowance 
Growth and Expansion Allowance in lbs/day, is the portion of the MAHL held in reserve 
to protect the POTW against expansion of current permitted industries.  
Per EPA Guidance, A POTW that anticipates growth in the future can consider holding 
a portion of its MAHLs for this growth. This expansion/growth allowance is separate 
from the safety factor.  By holding in reserve some of the MAHL, the POTW has a 
portion to allocate to accommodate growth or expansion of existing SIUs and may not 
need to revise its existing Industrial User permits or Sewer Use Ordinance due to the 
growth. 
Based on Best Professional Judgement, the BOPU has applied a Growth and 
Expansion (GA) of 10% in the final calculation of the MAIL.

14.0 RESERVE LOADING 
When a POTW allocates the EPA approved MAIL to individual industrial users, the 
POTW generally retains a portion of the MAIL in reserve so that new industrial users 
can be given an allocation out of the calculated MAIL. This also allows the POTW 
flexibility to release the reserve loading as necessary without changes to the EPA 
approved MAIL or changes to the Technically-Based Local Limits. Changes to the MAIL 
require EPA approval as a substantial program modification, and changes to numeric 
limitations require a public notice period. If the release of the reserve results in a change 
to the calculated uniform or mass based local limit, revisions to the POTW’s legal 
authority must also occur. The allowable industrial loading held in reserve may also be 
allocated to industrial users requesting special isolated discharge events as long as the 
MAHL and MAIL are not exceeded. The BOPU is holding in reserve 20-percent of the

15.0 ALLOCATION OF MAILS AMONG CONTROLLED SOURCES  
Per EPA guidance, “local limits can take many forms based on how MAILs are allocated 
to IUs. The designation and implementation of these MAILs, including the allocation of 
Local Limits Evaluation

loadings to IUs, are left to each POTW, as long as the implementation procedures do 
not allow the calculated MAHL to be exceeded and provide a reasonable method for 
making allocations to the IUs.” 
“A POTW may select any allocation and implementation method that results in 
enforceable local limits to prevent pass through and interference and to comply with the 
prohibitions in the Federal regulations. The POTW should choose the allocation 
approach that best fits its own situation.” 
“Ultimately, the POTW will want to allocate pollutant loadings in a fair and sensible way 
that does not favor any one industry or group of industries, considers the economic 
impacts, maintains compliance with the NPDES permit, and otherwise achieves the 
environmental goals of the program.” 
Consistent with the city’s legal authority and current BOPU local limits allocation 
method, the Uniform Concentration Method based on each MAIL and total SIU flow will 
be implemented. The uniform limits yield one limit per pollutant expressed as a single 
concentration applicable to all controlled users.  Separate local limits have been 
developed for both treatment facilities. 
 
16.0 LIMIT DURATION 
Per EPA guidance, “When applying its local limits, a POTW needs to determine the 
appropriate limit duration. The POTW may establish limits that are daily maximums, 
monthly averages, or instantaneous maximums. In general, a POTW should base the 
limit duration on the type of criteria – long-term or short-term – used to develop the local 
limit. However, most local limits will be implemented as daily maximums based upon 
two main factors: 1) the short-term nature of the event that the local limit is protecting 
against; and 2) the infrequency of IU sampling.” 
The EPA recommends the use of a daily maximum.   
Consistent with the city’s legal authority and current BOPU local limits duration, Local 
limits will be enforced upon SIUs as Daily Maximums.
