Foreword 
The intent of the Crosswalk Criteria and Guidelines is to provide a standardized reference 
for evaluating, selecting, and implementing crosswalk installations throughout the city of 
Cheyenne. It will also assist the City of Cheyenne with maintaining consistent treatments 
across all locations. These Guidelines are intended to complement existing City of 
Cheyenne design standards while ensuring alignment with all applicable federal, state, and 
local regulations. 
These guidelines may be amended to reﬂect changed conditions, public preferences, and 
incorporate improved methods or technology.

Introduction 
Purpose and Scope 
The purpose of the Crosswalk Criteria and Guidelines is to help guide City sta* in 
determining when and where a crossing treatment should be installed and how a crosswalk 
can be improved in the city of Cheyenne.  The objective is to guide consistent and data-
driven decisions that enhance pedestrian safety, improve infrastructure accessibility, and 
strengthen connectivity of pedestrian facilities, all while balancing and maintaining tra*ic 
operations.  
The Crosswalk Criteria and Guidelines supplement existing City of Cheyenne design 
standards and do not replace federal, state, or local regulations. 
 
How to Use These Guidelines 
These guidelines are intended for use by Cheyenne Metropolitan Planning Organization 
(MPO) and City of Cheyenne tra*ic engineers and planners. When a location is being 
considered for either a new crosswalk or crosswalk upgrades – due to public feedback, 
new construction, internal recommendation, or otherwise – this document transparently 
provides direction to all parties. These guidelines should also be used in coordination with 
Public Works, law enforcement, and other City departments or partner agencies as 
appropriate. 
Once a speciﬁc location is identiﬁed, this document should guide decisions through a 
step-by-step process. First, the ﬂowchart in Figure 1 should be used to determine if a 
marked crosswalk is appropriate, bringing together various criteria to be considered. If a 
crosswalk is determined to be necessary, or if one already exists at a location, Table 2 and 
the “Crosswalk Treatments” section will help provide direction about what, if any, 
enhanced treatments are recommended.

Crosswalk Deﬁnitions and Standards 
Crosswalk Deﬁnitions 
Title 31, Chapter 5, Article 1, Section 31-5-102 of Wyoming State Statute deﬁnes a 
crosswalk as follows: 
(A)  That part of a roadway at an intersection included within the connections of the 
lateral lines of the sidewalks on opposite sides of the highway measured from 
the curbs or, in the absence of curbs, from the edges of the traversable roadway, 
and in the absence of a sidewalk on one (1) side of the roadway, that part of a 
roadway included within the extension of the lateral lines of the existing sidewalk 
at right angles to the centerline; 
(B)  Any portion of a roadway at an intersection or elsewhere distinctly indicated for 
pedestrian crossing by lines or other markings on the surface. 
For the purposes of this document, the following deﬁnitions further outline crosswalk 
types: 
- Uncontrolled Crosswalk: A legal crosswalk across a roadway approach that is 
not controlled by a tra*ic signal or stop sign.  
- Controlled Crosswalk: A legal crosswalk across a roadway approach that is 
controlled by a tra*ic signal or stop sign.  
- Unmarked Crosswalk: A legal crosswalk that does not feature any tra*ic control 
markings, consisting of the area between two accessible sidewalks on each side 
of the roadway. 
- Marked Crosswalk: A legal crosswalk that features tra*ic control markings. 
- Midblock Crosswalk: A marked crosswalk that is not at an intersection, featuring 
tra*ic control markings to indicate that it is a legal crosswalk. 
Pedestrians are permitted to cross a roadway at any marked or unmarked crosswalk. 
 
Other Relevant Regulations 
Other sections in the Wyoming State Statutes pertain to aspects of pedestrians’ rights 
when crossing a roadway, either in a crosswalk or outside of a crosswalk. In addition, the 
City of Cheyenne has adopted a Code of Ordinances that applies within the city. Chapter 
10 of the Code of Ordinances speciﬁes regulations related to vehicles and tra*ic. Key 
elements of these statutes are copied in Appendix A of this document.

Design Standards 
This section is intended to provide relevant standards and guidance in the implementation 
of alternatives selected in this document. 
Manual on Uniform Tra7ic Control Devices (MUTCD) 
The MUTCD provides support, standards, and guidance for the type and design of tra*ic 
control devices used for marking and enhancing crosswalks. While the MUTCD does not 
establish warrants for marking crosswalks or providing such enhancements, it does 
include application guidance for Pedestrian Hybrid Beacons (PHBs); this guidance is 
included in the “Treatments Guide” section of this document. According to the MUTCD, an 
engineering study should be performed before a marked crosswalk is installed, considering 
criteria such as the number of approach lanes, the presence of a median, pedestrian and 
bicyclist volumes, average daily tra*ic (ADT), and 85 th  percentile speed, among other 
appropriate factors. 
The MUTCD also provides information on when new crosswalks should not be installed on 
their own, without other measures designed to reduce speeds and shorten crossing 
distance. Crosswalks should not be installed across uncontrolled roadways where the 
speed limit exceeds 40 mph and either: 
A.  The roadway has four or more lanes of travel without a raised median or 
pedestrian refuge island and an ADT of 12,000 vehicles per day or greater; or 
B.  The roadway has four or more lanes of travel with a raised median or pedestrian 
refuge island and an ADT of 15,000 vehicles per day or greater. 
 
National Association of City Transportation O7icials (NACTO) – Urban Street Design Guide 
The NACTO Urban Street Design Guide presents a vision for enhancing urban livability by 
improving accessibility for pedestrians, cyclists, motorists, and transit riders. It o*ers 
detailed recommendations for the design and implementation of both conventional and 
midblock crosswalks. Key strategies include: 
• Incorporating vertical elements at midblock crossings 
• Improving sight lines for approaching drivers and pedestrians 
• Clearly marking crosswalks and locations 
• Installing medians or pedestrian refuge islands 
In cases where guidance from NACTO conﬂicts with the MUTCD, the standards outlined in 
the MUTCD take precedence.

Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) – Guide for Improving Pedestrian Safety at 
Uncontrolled Crossing Locations 
In January 2018, the FHWA released a guide aimed at assisting transportation agencies in 
developing policies and procedures for evaluating treatments at uncontrolled pedestrian 
crossings. An update in July 2018 added speciﬁc guidance on the use of Rapid Rectangular 
Flashing Beacons (RRFBs). The guide outlines a six-step process for identifying appropriate 
countermeasures: 
1.  Gather data and engage with the public 
2.  Assess existing conditions and prioritize crossing locations 
3.  Examine crash patterns and safety concerns 
4.  Select countermeasures 
5.  Reference design and installation resources 
6.  Explore implementation opportunities and track outcomes 
Step 4 of the FHWA guide provides a table of recommended countermeasures for various 
roadway conﬁgurations, tra*ic speeds, and tra*ic volumes. Additionally, Step 4 includes a 
table outlining these countermeasures alongside the speciﬁc safety concerns they aim to 
mitigate, such as high vehicle speeds or failure to yield to pedestrians at crosswalks. This 
table should serve as a valuable complement to the recommendations provided in this 
document for roadway designers. The table in Step 4 assisted in informing the City of 
Cheyenne’s guidelines that are presented in Table 2  of this document.

Evaluating Candidate Locations 
Location evaluation is to determine whether a marked crosswalk should be added at a 
given intersection or midblock location. This step is necessary if there is no marked 
crosswalk at a candidate location. Location evaluation should be documented using 
standardized forms or GIS evaluation tools, and existing marked crosswalks may be 
reevaluated if conditions have substantially changed. The Candidate Location Flowchart in 
Figure 1  guides the primary determination for a potential crosswalk. Exceptions are 
explained in the Deﬁnitions of Criteria. 
To use the ﬂowchart, start at the top and proceed based on whether the location in 
question meets or does not meet the listed criteria. Continue until you reach the red box, 
meaning a crosswalk is not recommended at the candidate location, or the green or blue 
boxes, meaning the location is appropriate for a marked crosswalk and should proceed 
with the Treatments Evaluation. 
 
Deﬁnitions of Criteria 
The following section provides guidance on the criteria shown in the Candidate Location 
Flowchart and discusses supplemental criteria that may be considered. 
 
Location meets sight distance requirements 
The American Association of State Highway and Transportation O*icials (AASHTO) A Policy 
on Geometric Design of Highways and Streets  manual, also known as the Green Book, 
describes sight distance as “the length of the roadway ahead that is visible to the driver.” 
Sight distance should be su*icient such that a vehicle traveling at a given speed for the 
roadway can safely stop before reaching an object. In Cheyenne, roadways with speed 
limits of 30 mph or lower must meet the requirement for 227 feet of sight distance. For 
roadways with speed limits higher than 30 mph, AASHTO distances are in place. Table 1 , 
interpolated from Chapter 3.2.2 of the AASHTO Green Book, shows the minimum sight 
distance required at various 85 th  percentile speeds. All crossings must meet AASHTO 
requirements based on the measured 85 th  percentile speed, rounded up to the nearest 5 
mph. Field veriﬁcation is required and obstructions (vegetation, parked vehicles, etc.) 
should be mitigated before installation.

Location is 500 feet or further from nearest marked crosswalk 
Use GIS tools, Google Earth, or an onsite measurement to determine the distance from the 
proposed crosswalk to the nearest existing marked crosswalk. A distance of 500 feet or 
farther is necessary for a location to be considered for a marked crosswalk. This primarily 
applies to uncontrolled or midblock crossings; at controlled intersections, spacing is less 
critical. It is preferable to space out crossings at or beyond every block in a downtown or 
street-grid environment.  
 
Cross street ADT meets volume threshold 
Exceptions to the 1,500 vehicles per day threshold may be made with City Tra*ic Engineer 
approval. Locations where peak hour volume meets or exceeds 10% of the ADT should be 
considered for exceptions, such as school crossings. 
 
Peak pedestrian hour(s) meet volume threshold 
To determine whether a location meets this criterion, complete a pedestrian count at the 
candidate location counting the number of pedestrians present in the vicinity likely to use 
the crosswalk location. This generally includes pedestrians traveling within the inﬂuence 
area of the intersection, which may include pedestrians crossing legally and illegally 
upstream and downstream of the intersection. When data is available on vulnerable 
populations, consisting of children, persons with disabilities, and elderly, these 
populations should count as 1.33 people towards the volume threshold.  
Peak hour volume can either come from direct data or be extrapolated from 15-minute 
data. Latent or potential demand may only be considered when supported by planned 
development, new facilities, or documented land-use changes.

Proximity to pedestrian generators 
Candidate locations that directly serve an existing park, school, senior center, transit stop, 
or similar may be considered to raise priority when compared to other candidate locations. 
When a location does serve a generator, pedestrian counts should be collected during 
peak use periods, such as a school’s arrival/dismissal hours. Locations that serve 
pedestrian generators must still meet peak pedestrian volume requirements. 
 
Crash History 
Crosswalks can and should be implemented before there is a pedestrian-related crash. 
Crash history can be used to raise a candidate location’s priority, but lack of crash history 
should not be used to delay implementation. A proactive safety approach is preferred. If a 
location satisﬁes this criterion, a crosswalk may be implemented despite potentially failing 
to satisfy other criteria, as displayed in Figure 1. 
 
Other Criteria – Pedestrian infrastructure requirements 
Locations identiﬁed as suitable for crosswalk improvements should have existing 
pedestrian curb ramps. If none exist, new ones must be built in accordance with 
Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines 
(PROWAG) standards, including ramps, slopes, landings, and detectable warnings. 
 
Midblock Crosswalks and T-Intersections 
Midblock crosswalks are generally discouraged except where pedestrian desire lines are 
strong and adjacent crosswalks are more than 500 feet away. When crosswalks are 
installed at T-intersections, they should be placed on the lower conﬂicting leg and 
supported by appropriate signage or geometric treatments.  
 
Exceptions to Criteria 
In certain circumstances, deviations from the criteria outlined above may be reasonable. 
Any such exceptions must receive approval from the City Tra*ic Engineer and must be 
documented in writing with justiﬁcation. Cases not deﬁned in this guide should be referred 
to the City Tra*ic Engineer for review. Engineering judgement should be applied in all 
situations.

Evaluating Treatments 
Treatment evaluation is for determining what aspects of each crossing should be included 
or excluded based on several factors. This is a necessary step if the location evaluation has 
shown a need for a marked crosswalk; however, the presence of an existing marked 
crosswalk does not always indicate that its treatments must be modiﬁed. Treatments must 
comply with ADA/PROWAG accessibility standards, including ramps, slopes, and 
detectible warnings. Re-evaluation of existing marked crosswalks should be up to the 
judgement of the City Tra*ic Engineer. 
 
Geometric Treatments 
Before implementing a marked crosswalk, safety considerations should be made for the 
corridor, including: 
• Reducing travel speed 
• Narrowing or eliminating travel lanes 
• Reducing vehicle volume 
• Other tra*ic calming measures 
These considerations may improve safety for pedestrians but implementation is not always 
required. In addition, there are various geometric treatments that could be considered for 
new marked crosswalks but are not required. Drainage and emergency services impacts 
must be considered prior to implementation of each. The following section discusses and 
explains two geometric treatment options for consideration. 
 
Curb Extensions 
Curb extensions shorten the crossing distance for pedestrians, reducing the time they are 
exposed to vehicle tra*ic. These treatments also increase pedestrian visibility to other 
users. The treatments also have been shown to reduce vehicle speeds due to visually 
narrowing the roadway. 
 
Pedestrian Refuge Island 
Pedestrian refuge islands are a raised median separating two directions of tra*ic and 
shortening the distance pedestrians must cross at once, allowing them to cross the street 
in two stages rather than one. Pedestrian refuge islands should only be installed on multi-

Level A: Not Recommended 
Crosswalk should not be installed except as part of a signalized intersection with 
incorporated pedestrian phases. 
 
Level B: Markings and Signing 
The most basic treatment for a location that qualiﬁes for a marked crosswalk is markings 
and signing only – every marked crosswalk must have at least these treatments. 
Markings should be standard crosswalk markings and should match the style of nearby 
existing crosswalks. Refer to the MUTCD for requirements regarding crosswalk dimensions 
and applicable supplemental markings, if any. 
Signs should be sized appropriately in accordance with MUTCD guidelines. Signs used 
could include W11-2, W11-15, and S1-1, and placards used could include W16-7P, W11-
15P, W16-9P. Signs at crosswalks should be placed and paired in accordance with MUTCD 
standards, and advance signs should be placed and paired in accordance with MUTCD 
standards.

Costs to implement Level B treatment generally range from $7,000 to $15,000. 
 
Level C: Rapid Rectangular Flashing Beacon 
Rapid Rectangular Flashing Beacons (RRFBs) are generally designated for locations with 
higher tra*ic volumes, speeds, and more travel lanes. RRFBs increase the visibility of a 
pedestrian who wishes to cross.

Crosswalks should still include Level B markings and signage in addition to an RRFB. 
RRFBs should be mounted above the curb adjacent to the crosswalk, with push buttons 
placed in accordance with ADA/PROWAG requirements. Where a median is present, signs 
and RRFBs should be mounted in the median in addition to each side of the road. 
Costs to implement Level C treatment generally range from $17,000 to $25,000. 
 
Level D: Pedestrian Hybrid Beacon 
Pedestrian Hybrid Beacons (PHBs), also known as HAWK signals, should be used only 
where Level B or Level C treatments are not e*ective for providing safe crossings. The use 
of full tra*ic signals at pedestrian crossings is not preferable in Cheyenne. Crosswalks 
should still include Level B markings and signage in addition to a PHB. 
The MUTCD provides guidance on the installation of PHBs on major streets. Figure 3 and  
Figure 4 outline the MUTCD recommendations for when a PHB should be installed. Using 
the ﬁgure associated with the major street’s 85 th  percentile speed, determine the number 
of vehicles in the peak hour in both directions of the major street and the number of 
pedestrians in the peak hour. If the plotted point falls above the line corresponding to the 
proposed crosswalk length, a PHB is recommended. 
Costs to implement Level D treatment generally range from $60,000 to $130,000.

Parking Prohibition 
Wyoming State Statute 31-5-504 prohibits parking within 20 feet of a crosswalk at an 
intersection, and the Cheyenne Code of Ordinances section 10.52.020 prohibits parking 
adjacent to the curved portion of a “bump out,” which applies to curb extensions described 
in this document. These restrictions should be considered when determining whether 
supplemental parking markings and signage should be installed. Parking may require to be 
daylighted when new crosswalks are added. These changes should be implemented at the 
time of the crossing improvements.  
Decorative Crosswalks 
Decorative crosswalks are generally not recommended in the City of Cheyenne. All 
crosswalks must comply with MUTCD Section 3C and any supplemental markings may not 
compromise the visibility and uniformity of standard markings. Decorative crosswalks are 
typically not high visibility crosswalks which are considered the safest type of treatments 
for crosswalk markings. 
City Engineer Authority and Discretion 
The City Engineer shall have the authority to evaluate, approve, modify, and implement 
crosswalk treatments within the city of Cheyenne in accordance with this Policy, accepted 
engineering practices, and applicable federal, state, and city standards. 
Crosswalk treatments may be installed, modiﬁed, or removed at the discretion of the City 
Engineer based on engineering judgment and consideration of factors including, but not 
limited to, pedestrian demand, vehicular volumes and speeds, roadway classiﬁcation, 
crash history, land use context, and operational or maintenance constraints.

W.S. 31-5-602. Right-of-way in crosswalks 
(a)  When tra7ic-control signals are not in place or not in operation, the driver of a 
vehicle shall yield the right-of-way by slowing down or stopping if need be to 
yield, to any pedestrian within or entering a crosswalk at either edge of the 
roadway. 
(b)  When tra7ic-control signals are not in place or not in operation at a school 
crosswalk, the driver of a vehicle shall yield the right-of-way to any pedestrian 
within or entering a school crosswalk at either edge of the roadway by slowing 
down or stopping. 
(c)  No pedestrian shall suddenly leave a curb or other place of safety and walk or 
run into the path of a vehicle which is so close as to constitute an immediate 
hazard. 
(d)  Subsection (a) of this section does not apply under the conditions stated in W.S. 
31-5-603(b). 
(e)  Whenever any vehicle is stopped at a marked crosswalk or at any unmarked 
crosswalk at an intersection to permit a pedestrian to cross the roadway, the 
driver of any other vehicle approaching from the rear shall not overtake and pass 
the stopped vehicle.
