# THE ZOOM CALLER
## A narrative of the record — Cheyenne, Wyoming, 2025–2026

*Being the story contained in the workspace volumes I–VI, told as it happened. Dialogue in quotation marks is drawn from the record — transcripts, agendas, and the page log — or dramatized faithfully from them. Private citizens uninvolved in public acts are blurred or unnamed. A chapter-by-chapter concordance to the sources follows the epilogue.*

---

### PROLOGUE

The app woke him at 1:52 a.m.

Orange on the western horizon, west of town, out past the belt of ranches where the interstate drops toward the summit. A pipeline venting fire beside a rail line. A train stopped dead on the tracks. The scanner traffic came in fragments, the way it always did: *rupture… impingement… hazmat… do not approach.*

He lived on the county line, in a house guarded by cameras and a dead man's trust, and he did what he had been doing for a year whenever the county bled: he posted. *Friends in Laramie County,* he wrote. *Reports of Union Pacific train fire with hazardous poisonous gases. Be on high defense.*

No phone buzzed in the county that night. No alert, no siren, no knock. At dawn, a fire chief stood in front of cameras and said the words that would cost the city everything, two summers running:

"At no time was there a threat to public safety."

Charles Miller saved the clip. He saved everything.

## PART ONE — THE GRIEVANCE ENGINE

### One

The confession came first, before any of it, in the spring of 2025, in a comment thread the size of a backyard fence. Someone had accused him of writing like a machine. He could have denied it. Instead he typed the sentence that would follow him for the rest of this story:

"The AI I'm using to assist me with my postings/responses, etc., generated it."

He was not ashamed of the tool. He wrote a treatise that June, naming the model, laying down limits for a man who meant to argue his own causes in his own words: the machine drafts, the man verifies, the record quotes the statute. He was a DoorDash driver with a seminar habit, a trust beneficiary on the Roundtop road, a burglary victim teaching himself law between deliveries. He had time. Time was the only asset he had in bulk.

### Two

The burglary was August 6, 2025. They took things his family had left in the country for three generations, and the homeowners' association's answer was a shrug with dues attached. That was the first lesson: *the paperwork protects the people who write it.*

He wrote some.

### Three

Three days after the fire, the city council passed an ordinance against noise. No decibel number in it, just a policeman's opinion wearing a law's clothes. Miller spent the weekend with the Fourteenth Amendment and mailed the council a memorandum, *FROM: A Private Citizen*, laying out the doctrine — fair notice, arbitrary enforcement, the whole line of cases — and told them what it would cost them. They passed it anyway. Nine votes, give or take, and not one of them asked the question the memo had asked.

He wrote the sentence that night that the whole story hangs on: *a right you don't have the resources to defend is not a right at all — it is merely a suggestion.*

Then he set about becoming the resource.

### Four

The neighbor called him a bot in November. *ChatBOT legalese,* she wrote, on the county-road page, and ten months before a councilman would say it into a live microphone, Miller learned the accusation would follow the method forever. He answered the way he always answered — the data is the data — and kept posting.

### Five

The cameras came in quietly, on the consent agenda, the Tuesday before Thanksgiving 2024. Consent agenda: the city's phrase for *nobody has to talk about it.* Twenty-three license-plate readers, $146,390, a purchase order and two grants and zero minutes of debate. In thirty days they would read 139,176 vehicles and call it safety.

Nobody talked about it. That was the point of the list.

### Six

The warrant ordinance was where he learned he could win.

The city wanted to send inspectors into houses when consent was refused — *determine, discover, or verify,* the draft said — and for two winters the council chambers filled with citizens saying versions of *no.* Miller said it with case citations. In February the language came back to the committee and two words were gone, struck clean: *determine* and *discover.* The city could verify what it knew. It could no longer go looking for what it didn't.

His win. Direct result, floor to envelope. The clerk's minutes rendered his name wrong that night — one of a dozen spellings the record would give him, as-transcribed, left uncorrected on principle — and he let it stand. The minutes are the city's confession, he'd decided. You don't edit a confession.

He walked out of that war with the wrong lesson and the right one, and it took the rest of this story to sort which was which. The right one: pressure works. The wrong one: they'll always fold.

They weren't always going to fold.

## PART TWO — THE ORGAN

### Seven

In February 2026 he carpet-bombed. Twenty surfaces in one night — the governor, the agencies, the comment sections of the willing and the unready — and then, days later, nine posts in four minutes, machine-gun rhythm, each one citing page and book and timestamp.

A man distributing fliers had a distribution problem. He solved it the way the century solves everything.

On March 30 he built the machine: a Facebook page, one admin, no co-authors, category *consulting agency.* He called it The Real Windy City, which was a joke about a nickname, which was itself a joke about the wind.

The organ had a house rule from the first post: *you cannot fight what you cannot see.* So it showed people. Agendas, every item, every appendix, links down to the city's own document server.

### Eight

April 13, the council took its first reading of the Cox Ranch annexation — twelve hundred acres of historic ranch west of Roundtop, under contract to a developer contemplating a data center the size of a small town's footprint.

A planner named Connor White, under questioning, said the truest sentence any official would say all year: "There's no mailed notice. It doesn't go through a public process. There's no discussion."

The mayor heard the danger in it and leaned over the truth like a man leaning over a hole. This *is* the hearing, he said. Right now. Tonight.

They would remember that, later, in a federal courthouse that didn't exist yet.

### Nine

April 20, one week later, the same ordinance in committee. The developer talked data centers at length, and the economic-development chief talked data centers at length, and then a citizen put the water numbers on the record and the chairwoman's gavel came down like weather:

"So I do want to say to the group, this issue is not about data centers. This is about the annexation."

Miller typed for eleven minutes and posted the patches — four scripts, fill-in-the-blank, each one ending in a question the committee would have to answer on tape. He was no longer arguing at the podium. He was publishing the podium.

### Ten

April 21, Finance Committee, the DDA's hundred-and-thirty-thousand-dollar communications plan — money already spent, now being laundered through a budget increase — and a councilman named Larry Wolfe had run out of patience and out of arguments in that order.

"I don't even know whether Mr. Miller is real or a bot," he said, into the live microphone, into the archive, into history. And then: "so that we can actually verify that he's not just a chat GPT robot."

The clip traveled. By June the reel had sixteen thousand views. A councilman had stood up in public and answered a spreadsheet with an exorcism.

That same night, Mark Moody — no ally, no liberal, no friend of the caller — voted no on the DDA increase and said the words Miller had been saying for a year: back to basics, sidewalks before sizzle reels. One vote. The first crack in the wall, and it came from inside the wall.

### Eleven

April 27 ran four hours, and it had everything.

The annexation died into a postponement — September, the developer's lawyer asked for August, the surveys weren't real yet, the mausoleum of a founding family sat somewhere inside the legal description like a bone in a fish. "What we need," the family's lawyer said, "is something in place that will protect that mausoleum." Miller stood in the Zoom queue and called the maps what he called them — *false* — and the mayor stopped the meeting to fight him, live, in front of the farmers.

"I want to correct the record," the mayor said, "so that when people hear what you're saying they realize what you're saying is incorrect, sir."

A councilwoman asked him, kindly, fatally, who he thought he was: "You seem to know an awful lot about an awful lot of subjects."

"My background is in systems architecture," he said. "In my field, we don't look at subjects. We look at data integrity. If the input is high fidelity, the output is reliable. If the input is approximate or unsurveyed, the system fails."

Later, past ten o'clock, gavel-worn and human, the mayor mocked him one more time — "Tell ChatGBT you got to get focused" — and Miller took the jab the way the record shows he took all of them: no raised voice, one sentence, on to the exhibit.

"Sarcasm will not fill the water tanks," he said, "and Robert's Rules will not fix a broken survey."

## PART THREE — THE SUMMER OF THE VOID

### Twelve

May 26, the gag proposal. One item per commenter, the councilman said. "You get to choose it, but only one… choose your words carefully and very few of them."

And the mayor, when the caller fumbled a mute button: "You cost you a minute and fifteen seconds while you're trying to find your chat GBT."

The room laughed. Court reporters do not laugh.

### Thirteen

June 1, the Planning Commission — the citizens' board, the volunteers — voted the zoning down. No water data, no designs, nothing to weigh. The professionals said *no* first. Remember that. It matters in the last chapter.

### Fourteen

June, the hearings. The developer's land manager flew in and told the truth under oath of public record, because it was the only thing left to tell:

"Given the early stage of this effort, Microsoft has not yet advanced detailed design for the campus… I do not have any of that information at my disposal."

No designs. No telemetry. No water numbers. A vote to rezone three thousand acres of ranch around a shape that did not exist yet, on the promise that the shape would explain itself later.

A councilman and retired doctor told Miller the California case didn't reach Wyoming. The record preserves it as he heard it, and preserves, too, what no record can: whether the city attorney, behind the closed door afterward, said otherwise.

### Fifteen

June 19, the organ detonated its first original weapon: video from a January retreat nobody had watched, a councilman and the council presidency, miked and unguarded.

"I think we need to start spoon feeding stories to the media."

And the brainstorm after it — journalism students, hired by the piece, to write what the city needed written. The reel ran sixteen thousand views. The hashtag was a diet plan: *stop eating from their spoon.*

June 20, 7:35 p.m., the organ shared its library in a single minute — the warrant wars, the camera audits, the pipeline forensics, the whole written arsenal — twenty documents, one burst, a canon published in the time it takes to microwave a burrito.

### Sixteen

June 22, the planner explained the agricultural zone the annexation would carry on its way to becoming a business park: "It's a backup zone." A placeholder. A legal fiction the size of a ranch.

Miller put it in the exhibit list. Everything went in the exhibit list.

### Seventeen

July 13, the finale. The council overrode its own Planning Commission and zoned the void. Seven votes, more or less, for a project that was, on the record, a rumor with a surveyor's deposit.

By the weekend there was a card table outside the courthouse, and a woman named Katherine McAdams and an old commissioner named Hasenauer, and a clipboard, and a statute — ten percent of the registered voters, twenty days from publication, the ordinance set forth in full.

Miller didn't hold the clipboard. He'd built the reason for it.

### Eighteen

The signatures came slow in the heat. Sparks and Lincolnway, the corner by King Soopers, the west entrance by the college, a man posting his street corner like a fisherman posting a hole: *if you see the yellow sign, I'm not far away.*

At the Capitol, sixty people and a long-shot candidate heard a rancher call data centers "a digital prison." In a Facebook thread, a woman whose well had gone dry asked who would buy her five acres now. A mother of two asked about the bacteria in the purple pipes and was told, in the same comment section, that no one had died, which was true, and that nothing had leaked, which was not.

The organ amplified the thread whole. It was the one time all summer the organ ran a fact it hadn't verified, and the thread ate it alive. The record keeps that too. The record keeps everything. That's the whole point of the record.

### Nineteen

July 27, the last public comment before the referendum clock ran its course, Miller asked the mayor a hypothetical: would you remove a citizen from the podium — a citizen, standing there in person — for pausing to organize his notes?

"If they're doing what you're doing, I am, sir," the mayor said. "So, let's go."

The organ set it in type beneath the words THE PODIUM THREAT, and the summer crossed from procedure into something older and uglier, and everyone in the chamber knew it, and nobody moved to take it back.

## PART FOUR — THE AMBUSH AND THE ARMORY

### Twenty

Friday, August 7, the petition went in. 3,300 signatures, give or take the clerk's arithmetic — against a threshold somewhere near 4,300, depending on whose denominator you believed. Nobody knew yet which it was. That is what the coming fight over the counting would be: a fight over the denominator.

Saturday, the city's website forgot the Monday meeting existed. The calendar skipped like a tape. Miller screenshotted the absence and titled it like a war map — THE CHOKEPOINT — and told the county where the hidden agenda link lived, because his house rule had never changed: *you cannot fight what you cannot see.*

### Twenty-one

August 10, 6:04 p.m. Roll call. Nine councilmembers, a mayor, and a Zoom webinar with one hand perpetually raised.

At 33:50 on the recording, the mayor told the chamber something was different tonight: "Normally I tell you that when we're done, we're just going to adjourn. I'm not sure that's going to be the case this time."

The board went behind its closed door for an hour and eleven minutes.

At 1:17:24 it came back out, and a councilman moved to give the city attorney everything he needed to file a lawsuit — against the petition, against the count, against the twenty days and the ten percent and the table outside the courthouse.

At 1:18:24 the mayor asked the room if anyone wished to speak on a motion the room had never heard of:

"I see none."

You see none, Miller thought, in a kitchen forty miles dark. *Nobody knew it was happening.*

He timestamped all three. 33:50. 1:17:24. 1:18:24. Three nails, one exhibit.

### Twenty-two

August 15, noon, the organ published the audit. PUBLIC NOTICE, it began, and then it wasn't a post anymore, it was a pleading that had wandered out of a courthouse: the statute, the timestamps, the $750 fine the law wrote for exactly this, per official, out of their own pockets.

And the three steps. Lock the audio before it's "corrected." Move to dismiss the lawsuit as a ghost — no lawful vote, no lawful client, no case. And watch the clerk's window while she counts.

The clerk's window. In the end — every election lawyer in every county in America knows this, and Grisham readers know it best — it always comes down to a middle-aged woman with a stamp and a statewide voter file, deciding what a signature is worth.

### Twenty-three

August 19, between 4:33 and 7:24 in the evening, the organ stopped auditing and started issuing ordnance.

Five posts, five stages, stitched like a belt of ammunition. The scandal sheet with every quote footnoted. The records complaint against a three-thousand-dollar estimate. The indigency affidavit — sign it at the clerk's counter, the notary is free, the filing fee dies at $160, the system itself will carry your lawsuit for nothing if you are poor enough, and the county is full of people poor enough.

Then the petition itself, all counts, captioned against the city, the mayor, the planner, the planning director, and a private engineering firm and its two agents. The rezoning was void because you cannot make findings about a void — the state's own supreme court said so, a Teton County case, 2012, the year the whole doctrine was born. The hearings were poisoned because you cannot mute one side of a controversy and call it a hearing. And the August 10 resolution was a ghost, dead on arrival at 1:17:24, because the sunshine law does not forgive surprise.

He closed with the doctrine, cold as an actuarial table: *when multiple citizens open parallel, zero-cost civil cases, title insurance companies will refuse to underwrite the land. This immediately freezes the project's funding in its tracks.*

Not to win the case. To make the land unloanable. He wasn't suing the city anymore; he was suing the ground's credit rating.

The forms were blank. The strangers could type their own names where a lawyer's signature would go. At the bottom, in the disclosure, the singular pronoun leaked out of the plural machine — *I am a technical auditor, not an attorney* — one man, in one room, admitting the whole armory was his.

There was one case he had not read. Every first-year knows it. The one that says a councilmember can vote a citizen's rights away in the dark and never answer for it in a courtroom, because a vote is not a threat and a legislator is not a defendant. He had not read *Bogan*, or had read it and bet around it, and somewhere in the city attorney's office, a light was on, and the light had read it.

### Twenty-four

The city filed its suit quietly, as requested, the plaintiff suing its own petitioners to save them from the arithmetic. The mirror was complete now, and everyone saw it at the same time: he pre-drafted lawsuits for strangers to file; they pre-filed one against their own. Same weapon, same factory, opposite muzzle.

The legislature of that little high-plains city had spent a year proving the caller right in the only language it knew, and the last proof was this — that when the counting got close, it did not defend the count. It sued the counters.

---

### EPILOGUE

August 24, 12:23 a.m. The organ posted once more, and it wasn't about Cheyenne anymore. Nuclear plants, waste hauls, decommissioning bonds, a whole state's balance sheet: *Who gets the reward, and who keeps the liability?* The county fight had been the prototype. The product was going statewide.

Out in the county the questions that decide everything sat waiting for September like cattle in a storm line: whether 3,300 was 4,300 by anyone's denominator; whether a lawsuit born in a closed room can die in an open one; whether the mayor's party survives an autumn of transcripts; whether the water under the ranch is a rumor or a measurement; whether one man with an archive is a nuisance, a citizen, or a weather system.

The clerk's stamp went up and down. The cameras on the county road read the plates of the curious. The organ watched, and archived, and verified, and waited for somebody, anywhere, to say something on the record that wasn't true.

At 1:52 a.m., out past the belt of ranches where the interstate drops toward the summit, the scanner app glowed again.

He picked up the phone.

*— the record continues —*

## APPENDIX C — WHAT THE STORY FORM SURFACED (the analytic upgrade)

1. **The bookend.** The story opens and closes with the scanner app at ~2 a.m. — the founding wound and the standing habit. The narrative proves what the volumes asserted separately: the archive is *grief with a filing system*.
2. **The true climax is the clerk's window.** Every volume ended on votes, suits, or pivots; the story form reveals the decisive scene is neither — it is a stamp, a voter file, and a denominator. September is not a legal event; it is an arithmetic event.
3. **Rinne is the tragedy.** President, spoon-feeder, dissenter — the only character who was right twice and wrong once, on tape, both times. The novel gives him what the analyses couldn't: a single arc.
4. **The filter failure is Chekhov's gun.** The one unverified amplification (Ch. 18) is the record's only self-inflicted wound — planted in Act Three, unanswered in the threads, waiting for a deposition.
5. **Bogan is the loaded chamber.** The armory chapter ends on the case he hasn't priced. The six volumes graded it D; the narrative makes it the cliffhanger it actually is.
