# Item 21 — Municipal Code §9.12.080, electric fencing — FINAL script
# (as delivered live 10/19/2026 ~27:46 on the meeting clock)

Charles Miller, item 21.

Staff reports zero comments received specifically on this section. But the same
packet contains a letter from Woodhouse Roden Ames & Brennan, dated October 5th,
on behalf of WyFresh Farms and the Kniseleys, which asks in writing for, quote,
"an ordinance that permits the use of installation of electric f[encing]." That
is precisely what item 21 does. I'd ask staff to confirm, on the record, why a
direct, on-point request for exactly this ordinance wasn't logged as a comment
under item 21. The Kniseley annexation itself reaches third and final reading on
November 9th — the same meeting items 15 and 17 reach final reading tonight's
farm parcels. If this ordinance is going to govern fencing on the Kniseleys' own
annexed land in three weeks, this committee should know that connection exists
before it votes, not after.

Now the part I'd ask you to slow down on, because this is a public-safety and
liability question, not a paperwork one.

Subsection A caps voltage at twelve volts for goat control, and requires that
fencing near a pedestrian path only be electrified at night. Subsection B — the
general livestock-containment exception that any qualifying landowner can use,
with no individualized city review — has no voltage cap, no energizer or charger
safety-listing requirement, and no day-or-night restriction at all. The only
standard is that it be, quote, "maintained in a safe condition consistent with
standard agricultural practices" — a phrase this ordinance never defines.

I'd also ask staff to clarify, on the record, what "twelve volts" in subsection A
actually caps. Standard agricultural fence chargers run on a twelve-volt battery
or outlet, but they output pulsed voltage in the thousands of volts to the wire
itself. If this ordinance is capping the power source and not the shock a person
or a child actually receives touching that wire, then subsection A isn't the
safety limit it reads like, and subsection B has no limit of any kind.

Finally, subsection B only requires the fence stay outside the right-of-way,
sidewalk, trail, or easement itself — it sets no setback from them. A fence can
sit directly on the property line, immediately next to a public sidewalk, with no
buffer at all. And where subsection A has the city approving specific locations
before fencing goes in, I'd ask whether that city approval creates any liability
exposure for the city itself if someone is injured at a site the city signed off
on.

I'd ask this committee to get those three questions answered on the record — the
voltage definition, the missing setback in subsection B, and who bears liability
under subsection A — before voting this forward.

## Outcome (confirmed live — this one scored)
Councilman Rinney, unprompted, on the record: "I hate to admit it, I think Mr.
Miller... brought up a legitimate question... should we have a voltage limit on
the fencing?" Director Bloom, on the record: "We can go ahead and investigate
what a voltage limit should be, and include that at the next committee meeting."
Passed 2nd reading unanimously regardless. NOTE: a committee member stated on
the record that the Kniseley attorney letter *was* included in item 21's
packet materials — if repeating the "wasn't logged under item 21" point later,
be aware staff may contest it; the voltage/liability asks are what produced the
actual follow-up commitment.
