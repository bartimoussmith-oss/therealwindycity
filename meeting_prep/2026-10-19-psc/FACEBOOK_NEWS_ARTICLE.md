# Two Cheyenne Boards, One Monday Night — And Every Item Traces Back to Something the City Already Tried Once Before

*By Charles Miller — Public Services Committee & Planning Commission, October 19, 2026*

Cheyenne ran two meetings Monday — the Public Services Committee and, later
that evening, the Planning Commission — and between them they touched a
parking ordinance, a livestock ordinance, an electric fence ordinance, a
gaming center, an easement with the Air Force, and a rewrite of the city's
data-center rules. On paper, six unrelated items. Pull the thread on any one
of them, though, and it runs straight into Cheyenne's own record — in some
cases, nearly two decades deep; in one case, just this year's headlines
catching up with themselves.

## The headline: a golf course becomes a 2,868-stall gravel lot

The Public Services Committee approved a one-year, no-cost construction
easement letting the City build a 36.5-acre Park-N-Ride on F.E. Warren Air
Force Base's old golf course, to serve Cheyenne Frontier Days starting in
2027. City Engineer Tom Cobb told the committee the lot will have 2,868
stalls — and when asked directly, confirmed on the record: **"This is an
aggregate parking lot... it is not paved other than the [39] ADA areas."**
Cheyenne Frontier Days is funding construction entirely; the City is taking
on the insurance, compliance, and site-restoration obligations, and the Air
Force can end the arrangement on 120 days' notice.

That arrangement isn't new — the mechanism is. City Council records show
Cheyenne has leased parking space from the Air Force at F.E. Warren for
Frontier Days almost every year since at least **2010**, when the City
first authorized an annual agreement for "a parking lot leased from the
United States Department of the Air Force, located on F.E. Warren." By 2013,
that lot had a name — the "South 40," off Missile Drive — and the
arrangement was renewed as a temporary, single-summer lease year after year
through at least 2019. In 2015, the City and the base formalized a broader
partnership: a Memorandum of Understanding for "reciprocal play" at the City
and F.E. Warren golf courses, plus a standing City/F.E. Warren community
partnership committee. That same golf course was still active enough in
April 2025 to register as one of the city's two largest water users in a
utility planning work session — meaning the land now being surfaced in
gravel for a park-and-ride was a working golf course barely 18 months ago.
The specific 36.5-acre buildout now underway, along with the companion idea
of a pedestrian bridge over I-25 to Frontier Days grounds, first surfaced
publicly in a Finance Committee meeting on January 21, 2026 — nine months
before Monday's vote.

## The electric fence ordinance tonight isn't the city's first attempt

Monday's committee carved out a narrow exception to Cheyenne's longstanding
ban on electric fences, allowing them for livestock containment under
certain conditions. What didn't make it into the staff presentation: in
**2018**, the City Council tried to repeal that same section outright —
Section 9.12.080 — not narrow it. That effort never even reached a vote.
After the Public Services Committee returned "no recommendation" at both
second and third reading, Mayor Rick Orr announced on August 27, 2018:
**"There being no motion to approve... the item died."**

Monday's narrower version passed. But it ran into exactly the kind of
objection that likely sank the 2018 version in the first place: the new
ordinance sets a voltage cap for one narrow use (goat control) but no cap at
all for general livestock fencing. After public comment raised the question,
Councilman Rinney called it "a legitimate question," and Planning Director
Charles Bloom committed, on the record, to drafting an actual voltage limit
before the ordinance reaches third reading next month.

## Wyoming Downs expansion follows a pattern that's seven years old

The committee also approved a special-use permit letting Wyoming Downs, LLC
open a second off-track-betting location at a former bar and liquor store on
Nationway. The company's representative told the committee this is the first
of "basically every OTB we have in the city" to go through the City's new
special-use process — meaning Monday's hearing is a template, not a one-off.

It's also not the company's first time under this kind of scrutiny. Council
records from **January and February 2019** show the City wrestling with a
different Wyoming Downs location, ultimately requiring Police Department
sign-off on a formal safety and security plan before a conditional-use
approval could move forward. Monday's approval passed with far less friction
— one public comment, "generally not in favor," per the staff memo — but the
underlying question the City asked in 2019 (what security a gaming location
needs, and where) is the same one that will recur at every remaining
Wyoming Downs site still to come through this process.

## Same night, same director, a much bigger fight: data centers

A few hours after the Public Services Committee adjourned, Charles Bloom —
the same Planning Director who just promised a voltage cap for electric
fences — was back in front of a different board, the Planning Commission,
to re-run the city's data-center noise rules. This was the only substantive
item on the Commission's agenda: **PLTA-26-5**, a text amendment creating a
dedicated "Data Centers" section of the Unified Development Code, postponed
once already from October 5 because commissioners wanted more on sound
studies, setbacks, and how differently sized facilities should be treated.

The headline number: the amendment would cut the allowable nighttime noise
limit at a residential property line from 55 decibels — the city's own
policy as of August 2025 — down to **45 decibels**. "This is a number that
we were able to get from national federal guidance," Bloom told
commissioners at the first hearing, citing World Health Organization
guidance on nighttime road noise. The amendment would also force rooftop
cooling fans into fully enclosed, acoustically sealed housings (staff says
these units are the actual source of "the majority of sound problems
experienced in the Cheyenne region"), cap emergency generator testing at 30
minutes a week between 9 a.m. and 4 p.m., and require data centers to use
closed-loop cooling systems instead of evaporative ones that consume more
water — the same question of water use that's dogged every data-center
fight in Cheyenne this year, from the Meta "Project Cosmo" water
contamination in March to the 3,460-acre Microsoft annexation to the
Orchard Hills project that Council finalized just two days earlier, on
October 17, over continued resident objection.

The amendment also quietly writes something new into the code: a section
barring City staff from ever signing a non-disclosure or confidentiality
agreement with a developer, and declaring that every document submitted in
a development review is a public record. Staff says this doesn't change how
the City actually operates — officials say they've never signed an NDA —
but it lands at the end of a year in which residents have repeatedly asked
how much of these deals happens off the record. As recently as September,
a different data-center developer, Skybox, told the Public Services
Committee it had no written lease with its landowner, only "verbal
conversations."

What's most striking is what's *not* on the record yet: as of the staff
report's publication, the city had received **zero public comments** on
this specific amendment — a sharp contrast to the standing-room crowds that
turned out for Orchard Hills, the Microsoft annexation, and this spring's
failed data-center moratorium vote. Monday night may be the first real test
of whether that silence holds.

## The common thread

None of Monday's six items — across two different boards — are really new
fights. They're the current chapter of ones Cheyenne has been having, on
the record, since at least 2010 for the parking lot, 2018 for the fences,
2019 for the gaming license, and all of this year for data centers. Even
the one City official who shows up in two completely separate committee
rooms the same night — Charles Bloom, promising a voltage cap in one room
and defending a noise cap in the other — is proof the same small group of
staff is relitigating the same categories of questions over and over. The
city's own transcripts are the best argument for reading the fine print
before the vote, because more often than not, somebody already asked the
exact same question years — or just months — ago, and the answer is
sitting in the minutes.
