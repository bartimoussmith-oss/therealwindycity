# THE PRICE OF A PHONE CALL
## Ultra-Low-Cost Intervention and the Private Production of Governing Power
### A Case Study of the Miller Campaign, Cheyenne, Wyoming, 2025–2026

**Abstract.** Between late 2025 and August 2026, an unidentified-residency private actor participating exclusively by telephone in the public-comment segments of Cheyenne, Wyoming municipal meetings produced, at a marginal cash cost approximating zero, a portfolio of governance outcomes that in market-equivalent terms spans five to low-six figures in dollars: a floor amendment to a city ordinance, the only killed land-use package of the annexation season, a chain-of-custody litigation record spanning 59 transcripts and 484 statements, substantial enablement of a 2,739-signature referendum that forced the city to sue for declaratory relief against its own electorate, and the documented output of a pre-built single-administrator media apparatus reaching five digits of viewership. Against this yield, the campaign's identifiable marginal cash outlay is the cost of telephone service; its full economic cost, including unpaid labor, is three orders of magnitude smaller than the value it created and destroyed. This report (1) situates the case in the theory of participatory bureaucracy, collective action, rent-seeking, and asymmetric conflict; (2) decomposes the campaign into ten operating multipliers, each of which converts a free or compulsory institutional feature into a private asset; (3) prices the input and yield ledgers under explicit attribution discounts; (4) models the target's response options as a game in which every available move feeds the campaign; (5) states seven preconditions for replication; and (6) publishes a falsification protocol with dated tests through November 2026. The analysis is built entirely from public records and is disciplined by epistemic tags separating observation from inference.

**Keywords:** public comment; administrative record; asymmetric political action; standing arbitrage; municipal annexation; deliberative democracy; rent-seeking; referendum; single-actor campaigns; record warfare.

## CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION: THE PUZZLE AND THE CLAIM

### 1.1 The empirical puzzle

On the night of July 13–14, 2026, the Cheyenne Governing Body sat for 7 hours and 44 minutes and adjourned at 1:41 a.m., having approved a 3,459.99-acre annexation and rezoning package — approximately 18–20% of the city's pre-annexation land area — for a Microsoft data-center campus. [RECORD] Every contested item passed. The most visible opponent of the evening lost every vote.

Nineteen days later, a referendum petition against the annexation ordinance was filed with 2,739-plus signatures. [PRESS] Within another week, the city council voted 8–1 to sue — to ask a court whether its own constituents' referendum was lawful. [PRESS] Within another week, the Wyoming Secretary of State wrote to the city: *"I am deeply disturbed… let the people of Cheyenne have a voice."* [PRESS] A single-administrator Facebook page created before the annexation petition existed was circulating hot-mic exposé reels with 16,000 views through two seeded local groups. [RECORD] And the legal-exhibit spine connecting all of it had been assembled, turn by turn, across 59 meeting transcripts by one participant: 484 statements, 132 interruptions, 20 months. [RECORD]

The puzzle is not that a citizen opposed a development. The puzzle is the **production function**: how does a single actor, with no filing fees, no counsel, no staff, no budget, no verified city residency, and — critically — no standing to sue, generate outputs of this class? Standard political-science resource models (Verba, Schlozman & Brady 1995) predict participation scales with money, time, and civic skills. Miller's case appears to break the model: he has time and skills, no money, and no formal role — and yet out-produced, in measurable categories, the paid institutional actors around him: the $50M-community-benefits motion's sponsors, the petition organizers, the planning staff, and, in the earned-media category, arguably the city's own communications apparatus.

### 1.2 The claim to be defended

> **C-1 (the Phone-Call Doctrine).** A public-comment regime that is (a) recorded and transcribed, (b) open to non-residents, and (c) attached to a proceeding with quasi-judicial review exposure functions as an *unpriced factor market* in which a disciplined actor can convert speech-time alone into certified legal exhibits, compelled official statements, legislative amendments, referendum energy, and media product — at a marginal cash cost of approximately zero, and at a labor cost roughly three orders of magnitude below the market-equivalent value of the outputs.

This is a claim about **institutions**, not about an individual. The individual is the proof of existence. The claim's normative weight — whether this market *should* exist, and what its existence does to deliberation — is deferred to Chapter 11.

## CHAPTER 2 — THEORETICAL LINEAGE: SEVEN LENSES ON ONE PHENOMENON

No single existing theory covers the case. Seven literatures each catch a fragment. The chapter maps each to observed behavior; §2.8 synthesizes.

### 2.1 Deliberative democracy and its administrative-skeptics

Habermas's public-sphere account (1989) treats unconstrained public reasoning as the legitimating substrate of formal institutions. The administrative-law literature is less romantic. Elliott (1992) characterized notice-and-comment as Kabuki theater — stylized performance, passion elsewhere. Noveck (2004) attacked "notice and spam": mass participation that changes nothing. Coglianese's evidence was bleaker: in one ~500,000-comment EPA rulemaking, under 1% of comments said anything original. Cuéllar (2005), studying three rulemakings, found the *lay public* was the majority of commenters and carried genuinely novel concerns — "demand among the mass public for a seat at the table" — but that agencies treat unsophisticated comments differently. Wagner (2010) named the system's disease *filter failure / information capture*: agencies drown in one-directional information supplied by regulated interests. The ACUS (2021) report formalized the comment taxonomy: "detailed submissions by sophisticated repeat players" at one pole, form letters and mass comments at the other.

**Placement of the case.** Miller is the rare specimen the taxonomies anticipate but almost never observe at the municipal level: a **sophisticated repeat player with no client, filing in the wrong forum on purpose.** He solves Cuéllar's sophistication gap with prepared, citation-dense comments — and solves Wagner's filter problem by *weaponizing* it: the agency's inability to digest his input becomes itself the exhibit ("the evidentiary void"). Where the literature asks "how do comments influence agencies?", the case inverts the question: **how do agencies' responses to comments influence courts?** The comment is not addressed to the agency at all. It is addressed to the record, and the record is addressed to a future reviewer.

### 2.2 Collective action and the unilateral provision of public goods

Olson (1965): large groups under-provide their collective good because benefits are non-excludable; small "privileged" groups can provide it unilaterally. Benkler (2006) showed that networked individuals can peer-produce public goods at radically decentralized cost.

**Placement.** The movement's collective good is a *litigation-ready record of governmental misconduct* — non-rival, non-excludable, usable by any plaintiff, any candidate, any future council. Olson predicts it goes unproduced (free-rider paralysis). The case shows the Olson equilibrium broken by a single unilateral provider who accepts zero monetary return: the labor cost is concentrated, the benefit is diffuse, and the provider appears to be compensated in non-pecuniary currencies (status, influence, purpose — G5 in the goal model). **The record is the client** (`miller-goal-analysis.md` §7.2) is Olson restated as litigation strategy: the public good is produced *for whoever later holds standing*, solving the provider's own standing deficit at the same stroke.

### 2.3 Rent-seeking, entry costs, and the unpriced comment slot

Tullock (1967): resources dissipated in contests for state favors. The rent-seeking literature prices political entry — lobbying retainers, campaign finance, revolving-door expertise — and treats low entry cost as pathological (more contestants, more dissipation).

**Placement.** The public-comment slot is a **zero-priced factor of production** attached to the state's own compulsory machinery. Three minutes of speech time is free; what it can *buy* is not: a certified transcript entry, a compelled staff answer, an official's on-record admission, a gavel ruling. No market clears this factor; allocation is by willingness to absorb process cost (waiting hours for one's item at 1:00 a.m.). The case demonstrates that when the factor's product is denominated in *legal-exhibit value* rather than *persuasion*, its productivity per unit is explosively higher than deliberative theory assumes. This is the economic heart of C-1.

### 2.4 Asymmetric conflict: the interaction of strategies

Arreguín-Toft (2001), in the strongest general finding in the asymmetric-conflict literature, showed weak actors beat strong actors ~63% of the time *when they adopt the opposite method* — guerrilla vs. conventional — and lose overwhelmingly when they fight the strong actor's way.

**Placement.** The city's method is **vote warfare**: majorities, procedures, counsel, patience. Fighting that way (building a voting bloc, funding candidates) requires exactly the resources the actor lacks. The campaign's method is **record warfare**: depositions, admissions, preservation, time. Every observable choice is method-opposition: he declines the office meeting (no transcript), declines to run (no vote), declines the ward (personal jurisdiction), declines private resolution (no record), accepts every loss (losses mint exhibits). The strong actor's overwhelming force — 8–1 votes, attorney rulings, time limits — is *converted into fuel* because in record warfare the target's moves are the product. Chapter 7 formalizes this as the response-matrix closure.

### 2.5 Standing doctrine as filter — and as arbitrage surface

Administrative-law standing doctrine exists precisely to filter out non-injured claimants (the federal lineage from Frothingham through Lujan; Wyoming's municipal analog, W.S. 15-1-409(a), limits annexation appeals to landowners in the territory, city real-property owners, and utilities). Wolf's June 22 floor ruling ("he has absolutely no standing") was doctrinally correct. [RECORD]

**Placement.** The campaign's answer is *arbitrage in two legs*: (1) build the record standing-agnostically (the record does not need standing to be admissible in someone else's case); (2) route the remedy through the one Wyoming instrument that needs no individual standing at all — the municipal referendum (W.S. 22-23-1005), whose "standing" is 10% of the electorate, i.e., **arithmetic instead of doctrine**. The 2,739-signature petition is the second leg executing on schedule. [RECORD/PRESS] This two-leg structure is, to our knowledge, untheorized in the participation literature, and is the case's most exportable insight.

### 2.6 Information operations at municipal scale

Modern influence-operation doctrine emphasizes small persistent producers, platform seeding, memetic compression, and pre-positioned infrastructure activated at the moment of maximum salience.

**Placement.** The Real Windy City page (created March 30, single admin, no ads, "Consulting agency," 91 followers vs. 16K-view reels) is a textbook minimal-footprint broadcast node reaching through group seeding (Connections Cheyenne; Boomtown or Meltdown). [RECORD] The hashtag system (#SpoonFedGovernance #ThePanopticon #CivicAudit), the "Fight City Hall for $0.00" action toolkit, and the oppo targeting of a named councilman are not organic grumbling; they are **campaign communications discipline**. The critical production insight is temporal: the infrastructure predates the triggering event (the Highlands petition), which distinguishes *response* from *plan*. [INFER — linkage to the comment operation remains hypothesis H-E]

### 2.7 Commitment, tripwires, and Schelling structure

Schelling (1960): the power of announced, binding commitments; the strategy of the "thin edge" — small precedents that decide large futures.

**Placement.** "The trap is sprung" (July 13, 7:40) is a public commitment device: it declares the record complete and pre-announces escalation, raising the audience's expectation of court action and making non-filing reputationally expensive *for the movement*. The precedent ladder (2.69 acres → 26.29 → 400 → 3,460) is Schelling's thin edge weaponized: the actor *said so aloud* at 1:03 — "the standard you apply to this 2.6-acre vote will be cited in the appellate record for the 30,000-acre votes" — converting a trivial zoning item into a tripwire. [RECORD]

## CHAPTER 3 — METHODOLOGY

### 3.1 Design

Single-case explanatory study with process-tracing over a complete 20-month documentary corpus; outcome-to-cause attribution managed through explicit discounting (§5.5) rather than narrative confidence. The case is selected on the dependent variable (extreme output/input ratio); external validity is handled analytically (Ch. 10 preconditions), not statistically.

### 3.2 Data

The corpus and five primary artifacts (§1.5). Turn census is anchored on user-supplied "sync-to-video-time" transcript indexes, which are authoritative over official minutes (minutes log *items*, transcripts log *turns*): 5/10/10/16/8/19 turns across the six Highlands-circuit meetings; 40 statements on July 27; 484 statements across 59 transcripts total. [RECORD]

### 3.3 Epistemic tagging

Carried over from Vol. I: [RECORD] (verbatim/official), [PRESS] (named press), [INFER] (argued best-fit), [UNVERIFIED] (movement-asserted, unchecked), plus this report's new tag [ESTIMATE] (analyst pricing with stated band and basis). Inferences about intent are labeled as such throughout; the commissioning bias toward intent-inference is disclosed as a design choice, not hidden.

### 3.4 Pricing method

Market-equivalent pricing: for each campaign output, the cheapest market instrument that would purchase the equivalent good (e.g., paid signature gathering for referendum signatures; paralegal rates for record compilation; lobbying retainers for ordinance amendment; outside-counsel bands for induced litigation). Bands are wide, bases are stated, and every line carries an **attribution discount** reflecting the counterfactual that some output would have occurred without the subject (the referendum infrastructure of Madrid/Hasenauer predates and coexists with him).

### 3.5 External anchors (new in this volume)

- **Participation scholarship:** Cuéllar (2005); Wagner (2010) 59 Duke L.J. 1321; ACUS (2021) *Mass, Computer-Generated, and Fraudulent Comments*; the public-engagement and repeat-player findings as characterized in those reports.
- **Signature-market pricing:** Ballotpedia CPRS series — 2024 average $14.87/required signature ($172.15M across 53 drives); 2022 average $12.70; 2016 California average $3.94 (LA Times); 2014 range $1.05–$6.44 by state. Municipal referendum drives in small markets sit at the low end; bands below use $3–$15.
- **Labor pricing:** paralegal $75–$150/hr; municipal-staff loaded cost $50–$150/hr; outside counsel $200–$500/hr — conventional bands, applied as [ESTIMATE].

### 3.6 Limitations

(1) No interviews; the operator's identity, motives, and finances are open questions (hypotheses H-A–H-E, Vol. I §17). (2) Single case; preconditions in Ch. 10 are analytic, not estimated probabilities. (3) The media arm's link to the comment operation is unproven [UNVERIFIED]; pricing treats it as ecosystem output with a notation. (4) Attribution discounts are judgment calls; bands absorb the honesty. (5) The Rinne "spoon feeding" quote, the $50K PR retainer, and "Sam Rae's" role remain unverified (Vol. I §19). (6) Press reliance for post-July events.

**Verdict on the slogan.** As a *marginal-cash* claim — "at the price of a phone call" — the doctrine holds through 10^3 and touches 10^4 at the weighted-band edges: three-and-a-half to four orders of magnitude separate the dollars spent from the market value produced and induced. The commissioning flourish is thus precisely correct about the *institution's* economics (the factor is free) and should not be over-read as a claim about effortless output (the labor was real: 600 hours of it). **The institution priced the factor at zero; the actor paid in evenings; the city paid in counsel.**

## CHAPTER 6 — THE MECHANISM INVENTORY: TEN MULTIPLIERS

The generalizable core. Each multiplier: an institutional feature that is free/compulsory to the actor, costly/compulsory for the target. Together they constitute the production function behind Ch. 5's numbers.


### 7.2 Why adaptation failed (2025–26 as observed)

The model's exits — the moves that would actually starve the campaign — were identified in Vol. I §13: adopt findings-of-fact recitals; publish substitutes in advance; answer substantive comment in written staff memos (unattributable to the dais, non-improvised); uniform identification rules applied to *all* commenters; refuse the reactive hostilities that mint M7. As of August 23, the observed record shows: findings still absent (the void argument's fuel), substitutes still late (26A), answers still delivered from the dais, and the July 27 chair still choosing combat ("that's not my job to help you with that… you've got to be prepared, sir"). [RECORD] The city instead escalated to the terminal move — suing over the referendum — which maximized the campaign's institutional yield (a statewide official's intervention; the narrative "they're suing the voters") at the cost of zero record-assets for itself. Each individual move was locally rational under vote-warfare accounting; the sequence was collectively self-defeating under record-warfare accounting. That divergence between two accounting systems *is* the mechanism.

### 7.3 The one argument that escapes the game

Intellectual honesty requires flagging it: Esquibel's counter-thesis — a successful referendum only pushes the project to "the county with less regulation" — is the single observed city argument that attacks the campaign's *premise* rather than its process. [PRESS] It does not feed the record; it reframes the referendum's prize as possibly negative-sum for the movement's actual goals. The campaign has no recorded answer to it. This is the game's one non-closed cell.

## CHAPTER 8 — THE LEGAL-INSTITUTIONAL ENGINE ROOM

### 8.1 The two statutes that shape the whole campaign

- **W.S. 15-1-409(a)/(c):** annexation-appeal standing confined to landowners-in-territory, city real-property owners, utilities; 60-day appeal bar from effective date (publication). → forecloses the actor's personal suit; sets the option expiry (≈ Sept 16, 2026, publication affidavit pending).
- **W.S. 22-23-1005:** referendum on 10% of qualified electors within 20 days of first publication. → the arithmetic remedy. The DJ action asks a court whether the first statute crowds out the second; the campaign's design hedges both (record arms any 409-qualified plaintiff; referendum supplies the 1005 path; the fall docket carries both).

### 8.2 The doctrine audit (condensed from Vol. I §15)

Survives contact: the quote-chain; the three volunteered confessions; the 26A ghost-document sequence; the override chain's checkable arithmetic ("zero new technical studies, zero updated infrastructure analysis, zero empirical data"). Vulnerable: the quasi-judicial characterization (city attorney: legislative; weight of authority with him); garbled citations as spoken; the multi-gigawatt figure. The July 6 Brodie ruling conceded the *substance* of the central doctrine while denying its name — the campaign extracted a doctrine-validation from opposing counsel, for free, into the record. [RECORD]

### 8.3 Why this municipality, and why now

The attack surface is *standardized*: Granicus/Granicus-style portals, YouTube-transcribed meetings, Connect-Cheyenne-type case systems, three-reading ordinance pipelines, PSC shuttle-mechanics — this is the national default municipal stack. Every element the campaign exploited (published transcripts; remote comment — the actor never physically appeared; portal document access; agenda-item numbering) is a *feature installed for transparency* that doubles as a zero-cost litigation-support system. The transparency infrastructure of 2015–2025 is the record-warfare arsenal of 2026. Chapter 10 draws the consequence.

## CHAPTER 10 — GENERALIZABILITY: SEVEN PRECONDITIONS AND THE FAILURE MAP

### 10.1 Where this replicates (all seven required)

1. **Recorded, transcribed, published comment** (the mint: M1/M4). No transcript, no corpus.
2. **Q&A permeability** (M3): staff/council answer citizen questions from the dais. Written-only comment regimes close this.
3. **Quasi-judicial review exposure** at some layer (APA-analog review, certiorari, de novo statutory review) — the exhibit system needs a future consumer.
4. **A dormant movement** needing legal vocabulary (the distribution layer must exist; it cannot be manufactured by the commenter alone — 91 followers cannot seed themselves).
5. **Broadcast infrastructure** at zero marginal cost (platform groups; the M9 layer).
6. **Procedural exposure in the target** (loose findings; late substitutes; conflicts) — the M5–M7 amplifiers need real defects to harvest; a disciplined clerk starves them.
7. **A standing-free remedy** (referendum/initiative/recall) as the second arbitrage leg.

### 10.2 Where it fails

Written-comment-only regimes (M2/M3 die); non-recorded sessions (M1 dies); strong findings-of-fact practice (M5/M6 starve); counsel discipline — written responses only, no dais improvisation (M2/M7 starve); no referendum instrument (M8 loses its second leg); and the brutal base-rate case: **an actor who lacks the discipline.** The venue is free; the method is not. The corpus shows the difference between this and ordinary gadfly output is not access but practice: preparation, chain-of-custody, register-switching, self-correction. Replication of the *venue* is trivial (every Granicus city has it); replication of the *practice* is the scarce factor.

### 10.3 Institutional implication (for the targets)

If Ch. 6–7 are right, the rational municipal counter-move set is: findings recitals on every land-use item (kills the void argument), advance publication of all substitutes (kills 26A-class exhibits), written-only substantive responses (kills admission-harvesting), uniform comment rules (kills selective-treatment exhibits), and — hardest — behavioral neutrality at the dais after 10 p.m. (kills M7). Every one of these is *also* good-government best practice, which is the report's most uncomfortable finding: **the campaign's demands and the city's own procedural hygiene are the same list.** The phone-call doctrine exploits the gap between what municipalities preach (transparency) and what they practice (improvisation).

## CHAPTER 11 — THE NORMATIVE LEDGER: WHAT THE CAMPAIGN EXTERNALIZES

Research grade requires the costs be priced alongside the yield.

- **11.1 The commons cost.** Meeting time is a shared resource. Seven-and-three-quarter-hour sessions, 1:41 a.m. adjournments, and 40-statement reappearances tax every other participant, staff member, and viewer; deliberation quality degrades late (Laybourn's on-record complaint: the process has become "personality-driven"). The campaign's efficiency is partly *paid for out of the commons*.
- **11.2 The equity critique.** 600 unpaid hours over 20 months is a leisure-class or obsession-class resource. A resident working two jobs cannot run this play. The doctrine's availability is formally universal and practically regressive.
- **11.3 The epistemics.** The record weaponizes error as readily as truth: garbled citations travel as fluently as the three real confessions; "173 data centers" vs. "93M gallons" vs. "5M gallons/day" circulate un arbitrated; the movement's strongest media asset (the spoon-feeding hot mic) is as-yet unverified. Record-warfare raises the stakes on verification for everyone, and the campaign has no internal referee.
- **11.4 The legitimacy externality.** Deliberative institutions run on performed respect; a method whose fuel is institutional reaction systematically selects for conflict. The city's counter-thesis (§7.3) — that victory may just relocate the project beyond the city's stricter rules — is the hard reminder that process dominance and policy victory are different goods.
- **11.5 The steelman for the subject.** Nothing in the record shows personal enrichment; the produced good (a certified record of official conduct) is exactly what transparency law says it wants; and February 2026 proves the method delivers *corrective* legislation (warrants belong with judges), not merely obstruction. The campaign is best read not as a parasite on deliberation but as *deliberation's unlicensed enforcement mechanism* — with all the legitimacy problems that phrase implies.
