# FRONT PAGE

# HE LOST EVERY VOTE. THEN HE BUILT THE CASES.

### Over eight months, a county man who attends city meetings from a Zoom window has assembled the most organized legal challenge to Cheyenne city government in modern memory. He has won once already. Three of his cases are strong enough to matter, two are waiting on events, three are dead on arrival — and one belongs to the November ballot now, not to him.

**By THE LEDGER STAFF**

CHEYENNE — Here is the strange thing about Charles Miller's year: he has never won a vote.

Every time the Cheyenne City Council has decided the things he fights — the inspection warrants, the big annexations, the data center zoning, the budget — Miller has been on the losing side, usually 6-to-3 or worse, sometimes 9-to-1.

And yet.

When the city wanted the power to enter homes and businesses on "administrative warrants," the final law that passed in February was visibly narrower than the law the city first wrote — because of a sentence Miller said into a microphone. When the largest annexation in city history passed in July, a referendum petition with more than the required signatures landed at the city clerk's window three weeks later, built on machinery his operation designed. And when the city hired a lawyer this month to ask a judge whether that referendum even counts, the constitutional section at the center of the fight turns out to say what Miller's side says it says — in the state constitution itself, in words almost nobody had read.

He is not a lawyer. He lives outside the city limits, which means he can't even sign the petition he helped build. He speaks in three-minute bursts, from a webcam, and the city's newspaper of record has described him, flatly, as "a frequent critic of the city's planning process."

What follows is the layperson's guide to the cases this man has built — nine of them by the careful count in the record — including the ones that are strong, the ones that are stalling, and the three his own file says he should not bother filing.

Because that is the other strange thing about Miller: he grades his own cases. And his grades have been right so far.

# PAGE 2 — THE MAN AND THE MACHINE

## How a Zoom comment becomes a lawsuit: the $0.00 machine

Most people who are angry at city hall write a letter. Miller built a factory.

The record — nine volumes of transcripts, meeting minutes, press clippings and social-media archives — describes an operation with a production line. Agenda items get posted to a Facebook page and read closely. Testimony gets drafted in advance, word for word, and delivered at the podium inside the three-minute clock. The city's answers get recorded, clipped, and turned into short videos. When one door closes, the next agenda item opens.

The economics are the part lawyers notice. Wyoming law lets an ordinary person file certain cases for free if they cannot afford the fee — the record calls this his "$0.00" model. Winning a records case in Wyoming pays no attorney fees, so a self-represented citizen who spends nothing and wins costs the city real money to defend. Multiply by volume — the record counts more than two dozen appearances — and the arithmetic tilts.

**The strategy in one sentence, certified by the record's legal audit:** *state cases for the remedy, federal cases for the fees.* Wyoming claims can undo what the city did. Federal civil-rights claims, if they win, make the city pay lawyer bills. He runs both tracks at once.

## Who he is — verified facts only

The record is disciplined about this, so this page is too. He is a Laramie County resident, outside city limits. He attends and speaks remotely. He is, by the city's transcripts' own count, one of the most frequent public commenters of 2026. The press never once interviewed him all year — fifteen news mentions, every one a quote from the public-comment microphone. He writes his own material and has said so. Everything past those facts — the grief, the scanner, the origin story the record's third volume tells — is his own account, and the record labels it that way.

> **BOX: THE THREE-MINUTE CLOCK**
> Every case in this edition was built inside the same strange constraint: three minutes at a microphone, or a webcam, once every week or two. That is the entire access the law gives a private citizen to the body that decides everything else. The nine cases are what a person can build out of three minutes at a time.

# PAGE 3 — THE WIN, AND CASE No. 1

## THE WIN: The Battle of Three Words

In January, the council was moving a new law: city inspectors could get an administrative warrant — a judge's permission slip to enter property — whenever an inspection was needed to "**determine, discover, or verify**" a violation.

Miller's three minutes, Feb. 9, as the press printed it the next day: *"'Verify' is a viable legal standard. It means you have evidence and are confirming it. 'Discover' is a constitutional non-starter... It creates a general warrant, a fishing expedition authorized by the government to find a crime it doesn't know exists."*

The councilwoman who had been sympathetic moved to strike "determine" and "discover." It passed. The newspaper's lead sentence the next morning: "In a battle over three words, 'verify' was the only survivor."

The law that finally passed covers four narrow situations, three city officials, and commercial property only — homes dropped entirely. His amendment moving warrant authority toward a real judge didn't pass, but the words did die, in consecutive sentences of the news record, attributed to him.

**The Ledger's file note:** This is why his own register lists the warrants as a *win*, not a grievance. A file that counts its victories honestly can be believed about its wounds.

## CASE No. 1 — The Records Case: "The Ghost Document"

**The plain question:** Wyoming's Public Records Act says the government's papers belong to the people. What happens when an agenda references a document — and the document never appears?

**What the record shows:** a referenced item the file calls "the 26A ghost-document," never produced; a pattern of slow and partial responses to his requests; and a state law with a strange shape.

**The strange shape of the law, translated:** an official who *knowingly* hides a public record can owe up to **$750** — but the citizen who wins gets **no lawyer fees**. A real Wyoming judge confirmed this last year in a different case: he found the state had acted in bad faith, fined the officials — and ruled he had no power to award the winner's attorney fees, because the law simply doesn't say he can.

**The point, in newspaper terms:** in Wyoming, winning the right to see a document *costs the winner money* — unless the winner spends nothing. Which is exactly the kind of law a $0.00 filer is built for. **Grade on file: A−, his strongest state case.**

> **BOX: $750 — THE PRICE OF A VIOLATION**
> Write this number on your hand. It is Wyoming's going rate for an open-government violation: $750 per official for knowingly hiding records, $750 per councilmember for an open-meetings violation. It is also, not coincidentally, the fine the city's own warrant law attaches to code violations. Small enough to budget. Large enough, multiplied across members and episodes, to matter.

# PAGE 4 — CASE No. 2 AND CASE No. 5

## CASE No. 2 — The Annexation Case: "The Evidentiary Void"

**The plain question:** when state law lets a city swallow land, it must officially *find* that the move protects the "health, safety and welfare" of the people in the area. Did the city actually find that — or did it photocopy the conclusion?

**What the record shows, step by step:**
1. June 1: the city's own Planning Commission voted 3–2 *against* the master-plan change the Microsoft data-center expansion needed. By rule, that failure counted as a recommendation of denial.
2. June 8: the council took a standard vote to *overrule* its Planning Commission — allowed by law — but, the record says, without writing new reasons.
3. July 13: after a 5½-hour meeting that ended near 2 a.m., the council approved the 3,460-acre annexation 6–3. The "findings" supporting it were, the record says, pre-drafted recitals — conclusions typed before the hearing, not reasons drawn from it.
4. And the sentence that anchors the whole case: a city official conceded the decision sat on an "evidentiary void." His own words, in the city's own transcript.

**Why this is his best case:** the Wyoming Supreme Court has a habit — the record cites real reversals — of sending zoning decisions back when the city's written reasons don't show real work. Miller's own three-minute version aimed at the same hinge before the lawyers ever did: *"You cannot check a statutory box claiming you are protecting residents who do not exist."* The mayor's answer that night — that the law protects "those who will inhabit the area in the future" — is, on this page's read, Exhibit B.

**If he wins:** the annexation's paperwork gets sent back for a do-over with real reasons. **Grade on file: A−.**

## CASE No. 5 — The Meetings Case: "The Back-to-Back Meetings"

**The plain question:** Wyoming's open-meetings law has an anti-sneaking clause: a council majority may not do by a chain of one-on-one conversations what it would have to do in a public meeting. Did the deciding happen in the hallways?

**What the record has:** smoke, not yet fire — a compressed June–July relay of committee meetings and full-council votes on identical paperwork; an August vote to sue over the referendum taken after a closed-door session; and one fresh Wyoming precedent: last year, four councilmembers in another city were fined $750 each for deciding a letter by passing it around, member to member, without ever meeting.

**The honest status:** nobody has produced the Cheyenne version of that paper trail yet. If one exists, the penalty math runs at $750 per member, and — this is the part the city's lawyers worry about — actions taken in violation of the act can be declared **void**. This is one of only two cases in the folder that could erase the annexation outright. **Grade on file: B−.**

# PAGE 5 — CASE No. 3, CASE No. 4, AND THE BIG ONE

## CASE No. 3 — The Free Speech Case: "The ChatGPT Robots"

**The plain rule, translated:** a city that opens a comment period may limit *how long* you talk, and may insist you stay on topic. What it may never do — under a 2017 ruling from the federal appeals court that governs Wyoming — is punish a speaker for *which side* he's on.

**What the record collects:** a councilmember calling commenters of his cohort "ChatGPT robots" from the dais; a night in May when his item's comment got boxed to one subject while other speakers roamed; an April evening when his turns were cut off for being "not germane" three times while other commenters finished theirs; a July threat from the podium — "So, let's go"; and, after his videos went viral, the city turning off livestream comments entirely.

**The city's defense, stated fairly:** time limits and germaneness are legal; slurs from officials are their own speech, not city policy; and he is a county resident, so any residency rule on the forum would likely be legal. His case lives or dies on the *pattern* of unequal treatment, not the insults. **Grade on file: B+.** And quietly: this is the only case in the folder that pays lawyer fees when it wins — which is why the file calls it "the fee engine."

## CASE No. 4 — The Fair-Process Case: "The Empty Record"

**The plain question:** was the July 13 mega-vote run like a trial or like an election? Because the law gives neighbors real procedural rights in the first kind, and almost none in the second.

**The trap, translated:** his side's own paperwork calls the process court-like (to claim fair-process rights) *and* calls it election-like (to claim the referendum). A judge will make the movement pick. The record's register says so on its own face — that is what separates this file from a crank's. **Grade on file: B.**

## THE BIG ONE — The Referendum Case: "The Clerk's Window"

**What the constitution says** — not a statute, the constitution, Article 13, Section 1(b), words the record's audit dug out this month: if voters equal to **10% of the last city election** sign a petition before the deadline, a city ordinance **"shall not take effect unless approved by a majority of the electors voting thereon."**

**What happened:** the council passed the annexation 6–3 on July 13. Petitioners — two named organizers, volunteers county-wide, machinery from Miller's operation — turned in roughly **3,275 signatures against a bar of 2,739**. The city clerk began counting.

**What the city did next:** on Aug. 10, behind closed doors and then by an 8–1 vote, the council told its attorney to sue — politely — asking a judge to decide whether a separate state law about appealing annexations *replaces* the referendum for boundary decisions. Wyoming's Secretary of State has already written the city a public letter warning against "undermining a clear statutory process."

**The stakes in one sentence:** either 3,275 Cheyenne voters get a November ballot question, or the biggest annexation in city history stands on the strength of six council votes and an empty evidentiary record — and the two cases on Page 4 become the only road back.

**And the watchlist case underneath it:** if the city's lawsuit names Miller as a defendant — the record has predicted this since spring — an entirely new federal case appears on the spot: suing a man for petitioning. Its trigger is registered and waiting. The file calls it "the loaded chamber."

# PAGE 6 — THE FIRE, THE DEAD CASES, AND WHAT HAPPENS NEXT

## NOT HIS CASE — The Pipeline Case: "The Night the Sky Glowed"

On the night of Sept. 21 last year, a natural-gas pipeline ruptured beside the railroad west of town and lit the sky for sixty miles. The record's third volume says that night — heard over a scanner — is where all of this began for Miller.

**What is verified:** the rupture, the fireball, the scorched train, the federal investigation, and — every official account agrees — **no injuries.** What is *not* verified: early claims that spread through the movement that people died. The record flags that claim as its movement's one self-inflicted wound, and the wire copy flatly contradicts it.

**The legal point nobody sees coming:** if anyone ever sues the pipeline company — and strict-liability law makes that the strongest case *against anyone* in the whole file — federal law forbids using the safety board's famous reports as evidence in court. The reports can tell this newspaper what happened. They cannot tell a jury. Any real case would have to be rebuilt from scratch. The file keeps this case on its shelf, marked: *strongest theory, wrong defendant, evidence not admissible, and not his to file.*

## THE DEAD CASES — The file grades its own failures

**The record's register refuses to dress up three theories, and neither will this page:**
1. **"Sue the councilmembers."** No — a 1998 U.S. Supreme Court case shields local lawmakers for how they vote. The file calls missing that case "the kit's most consequential omission."
2. **"The city should have warned us."** No — the Constitution, the courts have said repeatedly, is not a promise that government will protect you from harm; and Wyoming's claims-act walls off most of the rest.
3. **"The zoning was simply unfair."** No — zoning by elected officials gets the gentlest review there is. Fairness alone is not a lawsuit.

A file that knows which of its cases are dead is worth reading on the ones that are alive.

## WHAT HAPPENS NEXT — The Ledger's docket watch

1. **The count.** The clerk verifies 3,275 signatures against the 2,739 bar. If enough are valid, the constitution's own clock starts: an election called within 30 days, held within 90 — that is, a November vote.
2. **The lawsuit's caption.** When the city's suit is filed, read the defendants' names. If one of them is Miller, the loaded chamber fires.
3. **Sept. 14.** The postponed 1,260-acre Cox Ranch annexation returns — the evidentiary-void case's second exam.
4. **The findings.** If the annexation survives the referendum fight, the "empty record" case follows it into court.
5. **The federal safety report.** When it lands, expect the pipeline chapter to reopen — in the press, if not in court.
