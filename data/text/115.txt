Dad accused of murdering his 4-month-old begins trial

CHEYENNE – The murder trial of a local man accused of causing the death of his 4-month-old son in 2025 began Monday in Laramie County District Court.
Joseph Hanson, 25, faces a first-degree murder charge related to the death of his infant son on Sept. 8, 2025. The child, identified only as T.H., was brought to Cheyenne Regional Medical Center for seizures, but was found to have suffered three brain injuries and eight skeletal injuries once he was transferred to Children’s Hospital Colorado.
Hanson and his now-wife, Juliette Martinez, both stand accused of murdering the child via abuse.
The case against Joseph Hanson
Laramie County deputy district attorney William Edelman’s opening statement outlined the events of Aug. 7, 2025, as reported by law enforcement and medical personnel who came to assist the family. Edelman told the jurors that T.H. was very fussy on Aug. 7; as a result, Martinez left him with Hanson outside the room where she and their two other kids went to sleep.
According to Edelman, T.H. and Hanson stayed awake most of the night because T.H. was fussy. Eventually, according to Edelman, around 3 a.m., Hanson drove to his friend’s house to see if they were awake, leaving T.H. alone in a room. Once Hanson realized his friend was not awake, he returned home. Hanson was then picked up by his brother between 7 a.m. and 7:30 a.m. and returned around 11:20 a.m.; the children were alone with Martinez for that period of time.
At around 11:30 a.m., according to Edelman, T.H. began displaying signs of a seizure. Hanson called his mother for advice, and after she saw a video of the infant, she told him to take T.H. to the hospital. Hanson and Martinez then drove to CRMC, where T.H. was admitted and began receiving treatment.
Edelman said Hanson and Martinez left the hospital, and when medical staff needed to contact them, they could not. Once the Cheyenne Police Department was called to make contact with the two, Hanson was taken back to the hospital, where he did not interact with T.H., the prosecutor said.
Edelman said that T.H. was removed from life support a month after being transferred to Children’s Hospital Colorado. He added that the cause of death was homicide by unspecified means, and that the injuries T.H. suffered could only have been caused by another person.
Edelman concluded his opening statement by saying that after Hanson was interviewed twice, he told law enforcement that there was a possibility T.H.’s injuries were caused by an aggressive diaper change, where Hanson pinched T.H.’s legs together at the ankles, and pushed them up toward his chest.
The defense's case
Hanson’s attorney, Marty Scott, began his opening statement by going back to T.H.’s birth in March 2025. He and his twin brother, J.H., were born prematurely. T.H. was placed in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit, though his brother was not.
Scott continued by saying Martinez never formed a bond with T.H. because he was hospitalized. On Aug. 7, according to Scott, Hanson and Martinez had a fight that upset Hanson. Martinez then went to sleep with all three children, though she brought T.H. out to the living room where Hanson was at some point.
Hanson had been staying up because of the fight, according to Scott, not because T.H. was fussy. T.H. was in his bouncy seat at this point. Scott confirmed that Hanson had gone out when T.H. was asleep to see his friend and was picked up at 7 or 7:30 a.m. by his brother for work. He returned home around 10:50 a.m., but sat in his brother’s car having a conversation until approximately 11:20 a.m., according to Scott.
At 11:30 a.m., T.H. began having a seizure, and Hanson immediately contacted his mother, who advised him to go to the hospital, according to Scott. Hanson put his children in the car and, according to Scott, waited on Martinez in order to leave.
Scott said Hanson left to go to the hospital at 12 p.m. and arrived at 12:12 p.m., according to phone records. Scott also confirmed that Hanson and Martinez did leave the hospital to go home, and that Hanson left his phone at the hospital and fell asleep while putting his 18-month-old daughter down for a nap.
According to Scott, T.H. was not fussy while Hanson was home, and Martinez confessed to her pastor’s wife that she had previously shaken T.H. and stopped T.H.’s breathing. He added that Martinez was looking things up online while she claimed to be asleep during the day, including symptoms related to shaken baby syndrome.
“There is at least a reasonable doubt in regard to Joseph Hanson,” Scott concluded.
State’s witnesses
The first witness called to the stand by prosecutors was Denise Abdoo, a certified pediatric nurse practitioner and specialist in child abuse and neglect at the Children’s Hospital Colorado, who saw T.H. on Aug. 9, 2025.
Abdoo found T.H. to have suffered three brain injuries, including hemorrhaging, and eight skeletal injuries, mostly to large bones.
During Edelman’s line of questioning, Abdoo said that because T.H. was so young, the only source of medical history would be his parents. She added that Hanson was the person she spoke with regarding any medical history, and that the only information provided was that T.H. had been in the NICU once he was born.
“There was no medical history provided to me to explain these injuries,” Abdoo said.
Although Abdoo concluded T.H. would not be able to inflict these injuries on himself, she could not confirm when the injuries happened.
Abdoo was asked about T.H.’s death once he was taken off life support; she stated she was not part of this decision. She added that T.H.’s body would not have stayed alive without life support because of the trauma his brain suffered.
“His brain couldn’t tell his body to breathe,” Abdoo said. “His brain couldn’t tell his vital organs to function.”
Scott’s cross-examination clarified that “yanking” on T.H.’s leg could not have caused the brain injury, and that due to the amount of injuries, one can conclude that someone engaged in “violent” behavior with T.H.
Edelman once again questioned Abdoo, asking her to clarify if the symptoms would have been noticed right away by someone hurting a child. Abdoo said that although the injuries were not visible, babies will typically cry or react "abnormally" to being hurt.
The prosecution's second witness was Stephanie Faber-Webb, a paramedic at CRMC who saw T.H. on Aug. 8, 2025.
Edelman asked Faber-Webb for her impression of T.H. upon his arrival, which she described as “really kind of flaccid.” She added that he was “tremulous” or “shaking a little bit.”
Faber-Webb explained that the main focus when T.H. arrived was to get “the child to break free from the seizure,” which meant they used medications to stop him from seizing. Once this did not work, T.H. was intubated, though seizures continued.
At some point, according to Faber-Webb, Hanson and Martinez informed her they were going to leave. Faber-Webb only got Hanson’s number. She later attempted to contact Hanson; she also got Martinez’s number from the registration form and attempted to call her. Neither parent answered the phone.
Faber-Webb later clarified that she found Hanson’s phone in a conference room in the hospital. She proceeded to call Cheyenne Police for a welfare check, and once contact was made with Martinez and Hanson, Hanson gave verbal consent for T.H. to be transferred to Colorado.
Hanson went back to the hospital, where he signed paperwork allowing T.H. to be both transferred and get a blood transfusion, according to Faber-Webb. Edelman asked Faber-Webb to describe how Hanson was acting when she explained the seriousness of what was happening to T.H.
“He was very calm,” she said. “Not very emotional, just kind of sat in the chair.”
Faber-Webb added that she told Hanson he could talk to T.H. or touch him, and explained that sometimes, despite the sedation, a patient can sense the presence of another person. Faber-Webb said Hanson got up and touched T.H.’s foot.
The third witness was Erik Schartiger, the charge nurse at CRMC, who was working until 5:45 p.m. Aug. 8. Edelman asked Schartiger to clarify how T.H. was presenting that day; Schartiger described T.H. as having rapid breathing, a rapid heart rate, not being fully conscious and displaying signs of a seizure.
Schartiger continued by saying that the rapid breathing and rapid heart rate never went away while T.H. was treated at CRMC. He also said the family did not call an ambulance due to lack of funds.
Edelman asked Schartiger about when the decision was made to move T.H. to Children’s Hospital Colorado; Schartiger responded with “2:30 p.m.”
Edelman further asked when Hanson and Martinez left and if they had clarified why they were leaving. Schartiger stated that the two left around 4:30 p.m., and that Hanson said they wanted to pack to go down to Colorado with T.H.
Schartiger added that the two had not returned by the time his shift ended at 5:45 p.m.
Scott’s cross-examination simply asked to clarify that there was no attempt to get consent forms signed between 2:30 p.m., when the decision to move T.H. was made, and 4:30 p.m., when Hanson and Martinez left. Schartiger confirmed this had been the case.
The fourth and final witness called by the prosecution on Wednesday was CPD Detective Timothy Campolong, the responding officer to Faber-Webb’s CPD call on Aug. 8.
At the time, Campolong was a patrol officer and was called to do a “message delivery” to 100 Miami Circle on behalf of CRMC. Campolong said in court that he called Faber-Webb, who told him she needed to contact T.H.’s parents, adding that Faber-Webb told him the two had left at 3 p.m.
According to Campolong, he arrived at the address at 6 p.m. and knocked on the door. Martinez answered and clarified that she and Hanson had been asleep. Hanson was called to the door, where he told Campolong they had left the hospital to feed their kids, and he fell asleep while putting his daughter down for a nap.
Campolong added that Hanson’s reaction to everything was “abnormal,” although Scott objected to this observation, adding it was speculative. Campolong also stated Hanson did not seem to know how to spell T.H.’s name when asked.
Judge Peter Froelicher called for recess at around 3:10 p.m. Monday, following Campolong’s testimony, adding that the case was moving at a good pace and the jury is expected to have the case by Wednesday.
Hanson is considered innocent until proven guilty in a court of law. If convicted, he faces up to life in prison and a $10,000 fine.
