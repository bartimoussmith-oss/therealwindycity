Civility, quality of life top new Wyoming House Dem Britney Tennant’s legislative agenda
by
Garrett Grochowski

Newly-elected Wyoming House District 11 representative Britney Tennant (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The GOP had a strong election year in Wyoming, including in Laramie County, where the party won all but one legislative race. The winner of that race, newly elected House District 11 Rep. Britney Tennant, says that her victory and a strong national outing by Democrats are a signal that compassion and civility are more important to voters than ever before.
Tennant went up against Republican Dan McIntosh for Wyoming House District 11, which bisects Cheyenne east to west and makes up most of downtown. Cheyenne has had a Democrat in the district before, Mary Thorne from 2007 to 2017, but redistricting and shifting political sympathies had put the district in Republican control since then. Most recently, the district was represented by Jacob Wasserburger, who was first elected in 2024.
Regardless of the district’s history, Tennant said she had a strong feeling she was going to win.
“In the days leading up to the election, people were asking me, ‘What do you think is going to happen on Election Day?’ and I continued to say, ‘I think I’m going to win, and it’ll be by less than 100 votes,'” Tennant said.
That doesn’t mean she wasn’t in slight disbelief on election night. She did, however, end up winning by 54 votes.
Looking back on her campaign, she said she sees the victory as a referendum on the performative fighting and lack of civility that Laramie County residents saw out of their officials in every level of government. Going door to door over the last year showed her that what House District 11 residents want are leaders who act like neighbors, including with people they disagree with, she said.
“I think people have turned away from being civically engaged and politically engaged because they are turned off by the rhetoric,” Tennant said. “They are turned off by the hostility. They don’t like the way that they see their elected officials talking to and about each other. That has certainly degraded their respect for and trust in the office.”
That resonated with her, a fifth-generation Wyomingite. She said she wants to be someone constituents can have a conversation with. During a time in which people are craving community and connection, that includes the more than 40% of the district’s residents who didn’t vote for her. Her work inside Cheyenne’s capitol will reflect that too, she said.
“Good policy is not red or blue. I don’t think you make good public policy to serve one political ideology; good policy serves most people. That’s my priority,” Tennant said. “I want to make sure that I am looking at advancing and promoting policy that helps people, makes life better for people and allows them to live the kind of life that they want to live, and I want to defend against policy that hurts people.”
She’ll be a trusted voice for Laramie County, she said, always voting in its best interest. As a member of the minority party, passing legislation will be an uphill battle, but she said good policy is good policy. Tennant added that she is ready to work with the entire legislature to improve Wyoming.
Her legislative agenda includes introducing a universal or state-sponsored childcare bill, defending public education, improving teacher compensation and — as the founder of Black Dog Animal Rescue — promoting animal welfare. She recently joined a legislative housing caucus to help make housing more affordable.
She is also concerned with how the state can better retain its young people, an issue taken up by leaders across party lines for years. Wyoming has led the nation in youth outward migration for a decade.
“What I see is that the things that young people have been asking for for decades, we still don’t have them,” Tennant said. “There still hasn’t been a lot of movement in that direction. I’m not really sure why that is yet, but what I am eager to do is make sure that people who want to stay here and build their lives here, that they can afford to do so and that the job opportunities that they need to build those lives are available to them.”
“A lot of the constituents I spoke to are of older demographics, so they have kids that have already moved out of the state and they talked a lot about regretting the fact that their family has moved away,” she continued. “Some of them still do have kids here. They have grandkids here and they continue to be concerned about what the opportunities for those people are going to be.”
She said she wants a better Wyoming for the state’s young professionals; it’s part of why she chose the tagline “a brighter day for Wyoming” for her campaign. Her campaign signs also stood out among the bunch, ditching the red and blue that most signs feature in favor of orange coloring and birds. Regardless of winning as a Democrat, she said she is more interested in standing up for her entire district and making a better Wyoming for its posterity.
“It is the tenor of the thing,” Tennant said. “I hope to be an example for how people can engage in the legislative process and across the aisle in such a way to restore that sense of civility to our legislature and to break down some of those silos and that sort of vicious political red-and-blue rhetoric. If I can move the needle back in the direction of civility and dialogue, then I think that I will feel good about that.”
