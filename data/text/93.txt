Cheyenne Farmer’s Market’s $750K season powers local economy, poverty relief
by
Garrett Grochowski
October 26, 2026
October 26, 2026

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The Cheyenne Farmer’s Market, which operates every Saturday in the fall, generated around $750,000 in gross revenue this year. Vendors, specializing in everything from honey to jewelry to tamales, are ending the season on a high note. So, too, is Community Action of Laramie County, which hosts the market and takes a portion of the revenue to support its mission to reduce local poverty.
As early as 5 a.m. every Saturday from August to early October, the clanging of tables being unfolded and tents being pitched bounces off the high walls of the Cheyenne Frontier Days arena. Like a caravan or a wagon train stopping for rest, Cheyenne Farmer’s Market vendors form a large circle of trailers and booths in the CFD parking lot.
A few hours later, guests start filing in. Sometimes with kids or dogs, they peruse each booth, purchasing homemade or homegrown goods. It’s a community tradition for many people, CALC CEO Sara Vigil and CALC Farmer’s Market and Operations Manager Esther Gonzalez said.
“What we wanted to do was create community,” Gonzalez said. “Tents with those picnic tables, people stood around and we got music out there. People just sat around and talked about politics or religion or what their families are doing or just whatever it is. But you do see a lot of people talking with each other and communicating.”
Cheyenne residents shop for pumpkins and other assorted gourds at the Cheyenne Farmer’s Market on Oct. 17, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
Shoppers browse the Cheyenne Farmer’s Market on Oct. 17, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
Community and communication don’t hold the same meanings as they used to, Gonzalez said. Cellphones and the COVID pandemic have made some people wary of talking with strangers and going to community events. What the farmer’s market has done for Cheyenne in that regard, she said, goes even beyond what it generates financially.
“All of a sudden you feel like you’re amongst friends. These kids out there talking to everybody, getting them involved in what we’re doing. And the same thing just walking through, I see random strangers willing to just chitchat,” Vigil said. “Maybe it’s because Wyoming doesn’t offer us many opportunities to come together outside and enjoy the weather.”
Halloween scenes at the Cheyenne Farmer’s Market on Oct. 17, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
A cool-looking dog drools while waiting in line for a coffee at the Cheyenne Farmer’s Market on Oct. 17, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
The financial impacts are also considerable, Vigil said, especially this year.
“It’s the biggest market we’ve ever experienced,” she said.
Gonzalez, who manages the market, said that previous years’ markets generated somewhere around $600,000 in gross revenue. This year, however, more than $750,000 was raised. Most of that money goes directly into the pockets of the 70 or so vendors that participate in the market — local growers and craftspeople selling their goods directly to their neighbors.
“Some of them were just floored by how much money they made weekly. In fact, we had quite a bit of vendors that sold out by midmorning and packed up and left because they had no more product to sell,” Gonzalez said.
Shoppers learn about different pickle flavors at the Cheyenne Farmer’s Market on Oct. 17, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
What’s good for them is good for the whole of Cheyenne, she added. Even the vendors who come from a little further away in Colorado pay for hotel rooms and the county lodging tax.
“They come here and they spend the night in the hotels and they pay for gas here and they buy products here,” Gonzalez said.
Most importantly for CALC, however, is the 5% of the gross revenue that it collects to support its work. The organization has operated in Cheyenne for over 50 years with a mission to reduce poverty and build self-sufficiency through housing, healthcare and advocacy. It receives a considerable amount of money from federal and state grants, but those funds often come with rules attached prohibiting CALC from using them to benefit people who don’t meet certain income qualifications.
The revenue generated through the farmer’s market, however, is completely unrestricted, meaning CALC can use it to help people who may not qualify for financial assistance in the eyes of the government but who are also struggling.
“Just in our 5%, that probably represents close to a $10,000 gain,” Vigil said. “The 5% of gross sales, that is the tool that allows us to bridge those gaps and make decisions that can help everyone that is in need instead of being restrictive as to who we serve.”
The organization’s core programs, supported in part by that funding, include transitional housing and case management for low-income residents, employment and housing support for individuals transitioning out of shelters, a healthcare clinic ensuring care regardless of ability to pay, a 90-day housing program for veterans and more.
CALC also distributes $100 worth of tokens to the residents it assists to use exclusively at the farmer’s market, which Vigil said helps stimulate the local economy by drawing more customers to local vendors and away from chain stores and helps keep locals healthy. By replacing cheap processed foods with local options, Cheyenne residents in need are getting healthier as they battle food insecurity, she said.
“Our highest levels of chronic disease being diabetes and hypertension, it all travels back to what we’re putting in our bodies in the first place and the affordability of what we can purchase in the grocery stores,” Vigil said.
The market was so successful this year that the CALC team is even considering a holiday farmer’s market downtown during the City of Cheyenne’s Christmas lighting ceremony Nov. 21.
“In Cheyenne and in any community, we’re no stronger than our weakest folks,” Vigil said. “So really, helping each other out builds a stronger community for everyone.”
