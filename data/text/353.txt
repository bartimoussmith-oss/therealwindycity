‘Every dog teaches you something’: Cheyenne officer remembers longtime K-9 partner after passing

Cheyenne Police Department Officer Lisa Koeppel and K-9 Tyler (Cheyenne Police Department Facebook)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — Last week, the
Cheyenne Police Department announced on Facebook the passing of retired K-9 Tyler
. Her time with the department, helping seize over 1,300 pounds of drugs and earning the department multiple K-9 training awards, will be remembered forever. For her handler, Officer Lisa Koeppel, she’ll be remembered as a best friend.
Tyler’s career achievements are significant by K-9 standards. Her detection skills helped the department seize more than 1,300 pounds of illegal drugs across 400 searches. Before she retired from service in 2025 after six years of work, she also performed one physical apprehension and 59 non-physical apprehensions. Between her drug seizures and her de-escalations, she has saved officer and public lives.
“Her presence deescalates a situation to where force was not needed to be used on that person and they complied and gave up just by her being out there with me. I think that’s just a testament to the level of additional officer safety that these dogs provide,” Koeppel said. “They don’t just have one job. It’s ‘I’m going to find narcotics for you; I’m going to protect you; I’m going to deescalate situations; I’m going to find people; I’m going to track lost people.’ Things like that. She did good at her job. She loved to do it.”
Koeppel can’t estimate how many lives Tyler’s work directly or indirectly saved, but reflecting on her work the last seven years has reminded her that officer work is invaluable. When she feels down about how the police are perceived by some of the community, she remembers Tyler, who only cared about doing her job well and sticking up for her community.
Tyler enjoyed her short retirement. With Tyler being neither Koeppel’s first nor last K-9, the officer knows that too little structure at home can disrupt training and performance at work. That was never a problem for the well-tempered and easy-going dog, whose professionalism earned her the freedom to be a normal pup when off duty.
“I found out fairly early on that she had that very good on–off switch and I could let her go and just roam the house and run outside and it didn’t affect her ability to work,” Koeppel said. “Shortly after I found out she was good with people and could interact with people and let people pet her and enjoyed attention, but still was able to flip that switch when it was time to work. Even though her retirement was fairly short compared to what I would have liked to have seen, there wasn’t much of a transition because most of her life off duty — when we were not working — was her just getting to be a dog and having fun.”
Around the department, she was known for being a gentle soul until that switch was flipped and she became a “badass,” Koeppel said. The department will remember her for her fearlessness.
Around Koeppel’s family and friends, though, Tyler was exclusively known for her gentle side. It’s rare that a K-9 can be so well-adjusted that they can be brought on camping trips or trusted to play around kids. She was at home wherever she went and with whoever she met.
“She was down for anything, going anywhere, pretty much doing anything,” Koeppel said. “I don’t have kids of my own, but I have a close-knit group of friends. Almost every camping trip she was with me. My friends and their kids, she basically adopted them as her own. If the kids were out swimming in the lake or playing on a raft or something, Tyler would be out there swimming circles around them and herding them and just kind of like acting like a protector. That was always something that just kind of warmed my heart.”
K-9 Tyler (Cheyenne Police Department Facebook)
She had near-complete free reign at home, which makes her absence there even more noticeable. Koeppel’s first sight in the morning was Tyler nudging her to get out of bed. The pitter-patter of her nails as she ran circles around the house; her stomping her feet when she saw birds flying overhead; her nubby tail trying to wag when she was excited; her abject hatred for orange traffic cones — besides being the best partner a police officer could ask for, the goofy dog was just fun to be around.
“She hated orange construction zone cones and she hated overpasses,” Koeppel said. “Whenever we would be driving and she would see those cones, she would do the same thing: She would lose it. When we went under an overpass, she’d spin around in the back of the vehicle, and then as soon as we passed it, she’d be fine.”
K-9 Tyler (Cheyenne Police Department Facebook)
Besides those cherished memories and her own career as a K-9, Tyler’s legacy will live on in Uzi, Koeppel’s new partner. Though not as easy-going as Tyler, young Uzi has all the makings of another excellent K-9.
Earlier this year, the pair participated in the inaugural Laramie County Sheriff’s Office “Top Dog” Competition, which brought together K-9 units from different law enforcement agencies in the region to compete in four skill categories.
Koeppel and Uzi won first place in three out of four.
Training Tyler, who herself won top dog honors at the Wyoming Police Service Dog Association annual certification in two separate years during her career, made getting Uzi ready for the job easier.
“A lot of those methods and ideas I used with Uzi right off the bat,” Koeppel said. “Uzi’s training from what we call a ‘green dog’ that has no training to a certified dog was actually much easier and much smoother than the older method that we used.”
Working with Uzi has also helped Koeppel with her grief. Staying focused on work, continuing to help protect Cheyenne alongside her new partner, has been therapeutic in a way.
Tyler taught Koeppel that training an excellent K-9 comes from communicating in a way that’s fair to them and shows that an error is a direct reflection of their behavior and not Koeppel’s mood. That means that, when they are in a high-stress situation, they know what to do, even when it’s clear the situation isn’t training anymore. As a best friend, however, she taught Koeppel something even more important.
“Every dog teaches you something. Every dog has, for the most part, that unconditional love,” Koeppel said. “I think the biggest thing that she taught me, unfortunately, came from her passing: that all the grief and all the pain is a direct reflection of the unconditional love in the bond.”
