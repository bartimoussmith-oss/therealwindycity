U.S. Fish and Wildlife staff tumbles by a quarter in Director Brian Nesvik’s home state of Wyoming
Nationwide, agency staffing has dropped by 36% since 2024 and wildlife refuges, already debilitated by a lack of resources, continue to lose employees.
by
Wyofile
September 4, 2026

by
Mike Koshmrl
,
WyoFile
In 2024, the National Elk Refuge had 11 full-time employees helping to
feed elk and bison
, greet 300,000-plus tourists at its visitor center and steward 24,700 scenic acres just north of Jackson.
During the
tumultuous two Trump administration-led years
that followed for federal workers, six of those 11 staffers have departed. Three refuge employees described as rangers left their jobs, and so did a maintenance worker, heavy equipment operator and administrative assistant.
Meanwhile, no employees were added, at least through late June 2026. That leaves five full-time staff — just 45% of the prior workforce — remaining to care for a wildlife refuge that was
created by Congress 114 years ago
in response to bad winters when elk starved to death.
Although the National Elk Refuge’s staff has been hit particularly hard, the hollowing out of its workforce is a microcosm of how the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has fared overall. The federal agency charged with overseeing wildlife habitat and imperiled species for the United States has
lost 36% of its staff
, dropping from 9,957 to 6,333 total workers between 2024 and 2026. That is according to employment data acquired by the Center for Biological Diversity using the Freedom of Information Act.
“These staff cuts are a cruel disaster for America’s endangered species and wildlife refuges,” Noah Greenwald, the organization’s endangered species co-director, said in a statement.
Young bighorn sheep gather as a fresh dusting of snow coats the hillsides April 30, 2019, on the National Elk Refuge north of Jackson. (Ryan Dorgan)
Overall, the Fish and Wildlife Service lost 1,900 scientists, including fish and wildlife biologists, foresters and fire managers, Greenwald calculated.
“This brain drain will be hard to undo now that the Fish and Wildlife Service has lost so many expert scientists who helped save endangered animals and plants,” he said.
The data used in this story come from the Center for Biological Diversity’s information request. The agency‘s workforce in Wyoming, the home state of
Director Brian Nesvik
, fell by 25%, holding up better than most states. There were
64 Fish and Wildlife employees stationed
in the Equality State in 2024. Since then,
20 of those employees have left
, while four have been added. That
leaves 48 employees
in Wyoming, which is home to eight wildlife refuges and two federal hatcheries that are spread across more than 100,000 acres.
WyoFile’s inquiry to the Fish and Wildlife Service’s regional press contact received no response before this story was published. Nesvik, who leads the agency, could not be reached for comment.
Fish and Wildlife Service workforces in neighboring states have been hit even harder.
Montana-based staff fell by 40% to 116 full-time employees, and Utah’s workforce slumped by 32% to 50 staffers. The Fish and Wildlife workforce in Colorado fell 41% to 272, and Idaho’s dropped by 35% to 112 employees.
The Green River flows through Seedskadee National Wildlife Refuge. (Tom Koerner/U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service)
The modern era has been tough for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service well before
cuts were imposed by the Trump administration
. An audit of the service’s 573 national wildlife refuges conducted in the spring of 2025 found that
not a single refuge had ideal staffing and resources
.
“Capacity right now is at a tipping point that puts both the economic and the conservation vitality of the National Wildlife Refuge System at risk,” National Wildlife Refuge System Chief Cynthia Martinez told refuge advocates in April 2025.
Nearly a year and a half later, conditions have worsened, said Desiree Sorenson Groves, president of the National Wildlife Refuge Association.
“That hasn’t improved. How could it, if you lose 35% of your staff nationwide?” Sorenson Groves told WyoFile. “There’s no refuge that all of a sudden was made whole, that I know of. If there is, that’s a unicorn that I haven’t heard about.”
Sorenson Groves emphasized that the depletion of the workforce at the National Wildlife Refuge System has been ongoing for two decades.
“It goes back to the Obama administration, and it is just a straight downward dive,” she said. “It’s a free fall.”
The annual visits to national wildlife refuges per full-time employee in the nationwide system nearly doubled between 2010 and 2023, and the ratio continues to increase. (National Wildlife Refuge Association)
The total National Wildlife Refuge System staff topped 3,200 as recently as 2012, but fell to fewer than 2,200 by fiscal year 2025, according to data Sorenson Groves provided. Meanwhile, public interest in visiting the refuges has surged.
“People want to go to public lands — that’s growing, while you have staffing going down,” Sorenson Groves said. “They’re going in completely different directions. The amount of visitors per [full-time employee] has increased by 94%.”
Sorenson Groves did have some cause for optimism. An
audit of the National Wildlife Refuge System
led by Wyoming resident Josh Coursey, she said, is a “good thing.”
“I think it’s long, long overdue, to be perfectly honest,” she said. “Brian [Nesvik] was asked at the North American Wildlife Conference if he was doing this so that he could divest refuges. He was unequivocal and said, ‘No, absolutely not.’”
The refuge system’s been through rough stretches before, Sorenson Groves pointed out. There was a point during the mid-1990s, she said, where there was low staffing, “not a clear mission” and some “inappropriate things” happening on refuges around the country.
A broad coalition that united parties like Defenders of Wildlife with the National Rifle Association pushed for reform, and they got it. Congress passed the
Refuge Improvement Act
in 1997, and conditions improved, she said.
“Things were pretty bad then,” Sorenson Groves said. “Right now, the reason it’s not as bad is because we still have the law to guide us.”
