Continuing America’s 250th celebration: Cheyenne Chamber Singers open 36th season with “We the People”
Carlee Elders

CHEYENNE — “Hey, learn about the U.S.A. …” Ding, bum, da, da, bum.
Learning and memorizing the preamble to the U.S. Constitution is a familiar task in American history classes. But by the time students leave high school, they may have forgotten those words — unless they learned them through “Schoolhouse Rock!”
“And they put those principles down on paper and called it the Constitution … And it’s been helping us run our country ever since then …”
So begins the chorus, the preamble itself.
For many, learning through an animated music video about American civics is more memorable — sometimes leaving behind an earworm that’s impossible to shake. Music, regardless of genre, can have a lasting impact, which is exactly what Cheyenne Chamber Singers Conductor and Artistic Director Sean Ambrose is counting on for their first concert of the 2026-27 season, “We the People.”
Even though downtown has long since taken down its red, white and blue decorations, the singers want to remain part of the ongoing semiquincentennial celebration, especially since they didn’t have the opportunity last year while celebrating their own anniversary.
Taking place Nov. 8, shortly before Veterans Day, the concert is not a commemoration of war or specifically an honoring of veterans.
According to Ambrose, “We the People” pays tribute to America’s Revolutionary Era and the events, ideals and people who shaped the nation — with themes that continue to resonate today.
“The full concert is really a reflection of the thoughts and ideals of the revolutionary period, which was the goal here. And yeah, it’s kind of post-America 250, but it’s our nod to that ongoing celebration,” Ambrose said.
Taking a slight departure from the singers’ usual performances, all songs featured in “We the People” will be performed in English and explore historically significant pieces, rather than focusing primarily on contemporary compositions by living composers.
“Chester,” composed by William Billings, was a hymn that became a popular marching song during the American Revolution, expressing the defiance and perseverance of Continental troops against British rule.
“Remember the Ladies” was inspired by a famous March 1776 letter Abigail Adams wrote to her husband, John Adams, urging him to ensure women were not overlooked as the new nation established its government.
“Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the husbands,” she wrote.
“It’s really a great song … I knew nothing about it, but when I was doing research for this, it was like, ‘OK, can we get some perspective of someone other than Thomas Jefferson?’” Ambrose said, though the concert will also feature a few songs based around Jefferson’s writings, like “Testaments of Freedom.”
Another concert this season will focus on influential women in American music history, coinciding with International Women’s Day. The program, Ambrose said, will span centuries, from some of the earliest female composers in the 1500s to an homage to Dolly Parton.
In line with the concert’s patriotic theme, and a nod to Wyoming, the singers will also perform “The Divine Source of Liberty,” written by Jeff Lippencott, the Cheyenne Symphony Orchestra’s first-ever composer-in-residence.
Based on the writings of Samuel Adams, the piece was commissioned for America’s founding celebration, and made its world debut with the orchestra last year, which the singers accompanied.
Ambrose confirmed there will be a few others based around the signers of the Declaration of Independence.
For 36 years, the Cheyenne Chamber Singers have provided an outlet for young adults to continue their love of singing after graduating high school, when many choral journeys come to an end.
As a former music educator and a member of the group for 35 years, Ambrose emphasized both his frustration with that loss of opportunity and his determination to give graduates — as well as adults who simply have a fondness for singing — an outlet to continue or renew their enthusiasm.
“I try and make sure that they know — not just my choir, but other choirs here in town — that there’s a place to keep doing what you love if you loved it. Community music is not about the job you do; it’s what you do when you’re not working,” he said.
The singers also give away student tickets to each concert to encourage attendance and introduce more young people to the group. Ambrose said that’s one reason he particularly enjoys performing at Laramie County Community College.
Cheyenne Chamber Singers member Anna Smith shared how “fantastic” it has been working with Ambrose for the past three years.
She loves how Ambrose challenges her and the rest of the group to push beyond what they know for each concert, which, in turn, keeps her invested in performing.
Smith hopes that the historical significance “We the People” is highlighting helps attract community members who may not otherwise attend.
“It impacts me 10 times (more) to hear something through a song because you feel it in your core,” Smith said. “You can be moved to tears by music, and that’s the beauty of music. And sometimes history is maybe not everybody’s favorite subject, but to be able to hear it through music, I think we can reach a broader audience and celebrate such a big year for the country.”
