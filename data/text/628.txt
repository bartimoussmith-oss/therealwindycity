Union Pacific’s Big Boy No. 4014 and Living Legend No. 844, the world’s largest and fastest-operating steam locomotives, headed out from Cheyenne Saturday morning to take part in a first-ever holiday excursion and fundraiser for the Make-A-Wish Foundation.
The locomotives stopped in Greeley, Colorado, prior to heading to Denver’s Union Station, where they held a festive holiday celebration with Santa Claus, Mrs. Claus and several of his little helpers.
Big Boy was adorned with Christmas wreaths and lights to spread holiday cheer to all those in attendance.
This is Living Legend’s first public appearance in six years, and it’s a continuation of Big Boy’s 7,136-mile coast-to-coast tour of the United States to celebrate the country’s 250th birthday.
Ed Dickens, the senior manager of Heritage Operations for Union Pacific, and the rest of Big Boy’s steam team sported Santa hats to mark the occasion.
Dickens, who has led Union Pacific’s steam team since 2010, was charged with the two-and-a-half-year restoration of Big Boy, which was completed in May of 2019.
In an interview with the Wyoming Tribune Eagle before the trains left Saturday, he talked about the work that goes into making sure these machines are still able to function, while being over three-quarters of a century old.
“Our day-to-day (schedule) is a lot of work,” Dickens said. “This (train was built in) 1941, the other one’s 1944. These old steam locomotives just require a lot of maintenance. I think that’s what fascinates people. They’re just these iconic machines from a different world, a different era.”
He explained that in Big Boy’s second life, it has been pulling passenger trains and acting as a railroad ambassador, which wasn’t the designed or intended use when it was originally built in the early 1940s. Back then, it was built to haul heavy loads of cargo over high mountain passes.
Dickens also stressed that the work required to maintain Big Boy and Living Legend differs greatly from what modern trains demand.
“The steam engine just requires a lot of maintenance compared to modern locomotives today,” Dickens said. “Modern locomotives today are very advanced, very efficient, lots of computerization, lots of automated technology on them.”
However, he did note that in terms of steam locomotive technology, both Big Boy and Living Legend are fairly modern.
Dickens emphasized that while technology changes, overall designs stay the same — they can still pull big, long and heavy trains efficiently.
“The most efficient, the most profitable way to do that is with good modern locomotives, and that’s what the Big Boy and the 844 were built for back in the 1940s.”
One complication of the trains being so old is that parts have to be hand-manufactured because they aren’t available anymore. But that isn’t a problem for Dickens and the rest of his team.
“We can make just about anything we need on this,” he said. “We’ve got the old 1940s drawings, and we get them all set up, and we make everything — exactly what we need.”
Dickens explained that nearly everything is brand new because of the effort and care that went into the restoration process.
No. 4014 is one of 25 Big Boy steam engines commissioned exclusively for Union Pacific, with the first one being delivered in 1941. It’s also the only one that’s still in operation.
The locomotive was originally retired in December 1961 after traveling 1,031,205 miles, before being reacquired and restored by Union Pacific.
Dickens said he also relishes the opportunity to give people, and especially kids, the chance to see these steam locomotives up close.
“It’s an opportunity to connect with the past and to see what we used to do in the old days,” he said.
He hopes more people understand the burden railroads carry in regard to the nation’s economy and commerce, in addition to being an interesting and efficient way to move things.
Dickens said the past six years of showing thousands of children the Big Boy has brought him back to when he was a kid, enamored by all things locomotive.
“I remember back when I was a young kid, 6, 7 years old, dressing like a train engineer for Halloween all the time,” he said. “Heck, I went to school dressed like this.”
The Big Boy has given him the chance to provide that experience to kids of the next generation.
“Every day, that’s one of the most special parts of what we do is interacting with the kids and blowing the whistle for them, and they just love it.”
Dickens concluded by appreciating the fact that keeping equipment like Big Boy and Living Legend around is a way to preserve locomotive history, and he is grateful to be a part of it.
“It’s an honor and a privilege,” he said. “And there’s not a day that goes by that we don’t thank our lucky stars that the decision is made to continue making this run. And we’re the ones that are tasked with that, and we love it.”
