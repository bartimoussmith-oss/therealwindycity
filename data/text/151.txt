Cheyenne rejects flood mitigation funding mechanism despite 1985 storm repeat worries
by
Garrett Grochowski
October 27, 2026
October 27, 2026

(Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — A proposal to amend the City of Cheyenne’s General Fund policy, allowing transfers of up to $10 million to a stormwater runoff management account, failed a City Council vote Monday. Policy proponent Councilor Michelle Aldrich said the policy could slowly raise money to avoid major infrastructure problems in the future, while others on the council called it a “coffee can” account with purposes mysterious to the public.
Cheyenne’s General Fund holds all of the money the city uses for daily operations. Per city policy, the General Fund must have at least 120 days’ worth of reserves; Cheyenne currently keeps around 365 days’ worth on hand.
Last week, a resolution to amend the city’s policy for operating the fund hit the Finance Committee, the group of councilors that considers city financial decisions before the council, and failed to garner any support. The proposal calls for a review process of the reserves every year when more than 365 days of reserves are stored up. If the city decides so, it can move up to $10 million into an account dedicated to stormwater runoff management.
Confused about the policy’s purpose, as the city can already move to transfer excess reserves if it likes, the committee declined to support the resolution.
The resolution’s advocate, Michelle Aldrich, isn’t on the committee and didn’t attend the meeting last week to explain why she considered the resolution important. According to her, the city is about $60 million behind on deferred infrastructure improvements that could prevent a weather event like the deadly flood of Aug. 1, 1985.
Cheyenne City Councilors Michelle Aldrich, Ken Esquibel and Pete Laybourn at a meeting Oct. 26, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
“Since the flood of ’85, quite honestly, we’ve had some drainage issues in our community that have been challenging to say the least,” Aldrich said. “It has been reported repeatedly that our community could be at risk if there were to be any type of rain adventure like we had in 1985.”
1985 flood kills 12, injures 70
According to the
National Weather Service
, a seemingly typical late-summer thunderstorm turned into one of Wyoming’s greatest natural disasters in Cheyenne on Aug. 1, 1985. A “devastating” hailstorm struck the city, flooding Crow Creek, Dry Creek and downtown Cheyenne.
In just three hours, more than 7inches of rainfall was measured in parts of Cheyenne. 24-hour rainfall records were smashed that day. Hailstones up to 2 inches in diameter were also recorded, forming 1-foot-tall walls in some areas of town before being swept into 3- to 6-foot-tall drifts by wind and the deluge. 70 mph winds and a few tornadoes were also reported in the general area.
Hail and floodwater collect in downtown Cheyenne after the 1985 flood (United States Geological Survey photo shared by the National Weather Service)
In all, 12 people around Cheyenne were killed and 70 were injured. The event caused around $61 million in damages.
Stormwater management fund to solve infrastructure issues, Aldrich said
The city is reportedly aware that it needs to improve its stormwater runoff management. It would cost the city approximately $60 million to do so. If a stormwater utility fee was implemented as the city has discussed in the past, it would take around 10 years to collect all the money necessary to perform the work at one time.
To show that the city has “skin in the game” and isn’t happy to place the burden of raising the money exclusively on taxpayers, Aldrich said, the city could simply budget for the improvements. She compared it to a family’s emergency fund.
“I think in any good budgeting system, in any household, a one-year emergency fund would be considered good fiscal control,” she said.
The $10 million per-year marker was proposed to round up the number and make it clean; the city would really only need $6 million per year for a decade to fund the improvements. It would also relieve pressure from state lawmakers who have criticized local governments for holding excessive unspent cash reserves, Aldrich said.
However, the state gets its fair share of criticism for its “coffee can” funds, funds so specific that voters are often left wondering what purpose they serve, Councilor Tom Segrave said. He admitted the proposal was a noble one, but said he would be voting against it.
“It’s very confusing to the public where money is … and where it’s not. I, for one — and I’m not going to speak for everybody — but if there was a legitimate project brought forward tomorrow, I would do everything I could to support that, but to create special little accounts, I just don’t think that’s transparent. I don’t think that’s good policy,” Segrave said. “It’s very hard for the public to identify where our funds are.”
The City Engineer’s Office also doesn’t have the staffing capabilities to manage that much money per year, Engineer Tom Cobb said at the Finance Committee meeting last week. It would take hiring more staff, which the office is always considering, to put the money to use.
