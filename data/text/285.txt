Grizzly bear deaths in Yellowstone region climb to modern record high for third straight year
Grizzlies killed because they harmed livestock and bears that died from accidental causes like vehicle strikes contributed to the record. Illegal killings and natural deaths spiked, too.
by
Wyofile
2 hours ago
November 2, 2026

by
Mike Koshmrl
,
WyoFile
With weeks left to go before many grizzly bears den up, it’s already the bruins’ deadliest year in the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem in modern times, surpassing records set in 2024 and 2025.
Historically, regional grizzly bear death counts have been higher. For example, biologist
Frank Craighead tallied 120
of the large carnivores that perished in 1972, the year after Yellowstone National Park closed the last of its garbage dumps that scores of
bruins depended on
for food.
Over the decades that followed, wildlife managers and
landowners went to great lengths
to reduce conflicts. At the same time, grizzly mortality rates plummeted as the
population recovered
and bears spread out geographically — expanding their
range until 2021
when it began
to slowly recede
.
Wildlife managers call the uptick in bear fatalities somewhat predictable and
not a cause for alarm
, but the numbers of grizzlies that have died the last three years have each set a modern record. As of Oct. 21, 81 known grizzly bears died in the calendar year, according to a
database
maintained by the Interagency Grizzly Bear Study Team. And there’s at least one additional mortality that hasn’t been logged yet, bringing the count to 82, according to Wyoming Game and Fish Department Large Carnivore Supervisor Dan Thompson.
(Graph by Tennessee Watson/WyoFile, with assistance from AI)
This year’s total mortality tops past highs set in 2025 (74), 2024 (72) and 2021 (70). Although this year was a record, the surge in conflicts and related deaths for grizzly bears paled in comparison to the
“unprecedented” conflict-ridden year for black bears
in Wyoming and
neighboring Rocky Mountain states
.
“I don’t have conflict numbers yet, but it was just a more prominent [year] with black bear conflicts, definitely, than grizzly bears,” Thompson said.
In 2026, grizzly bears in the Yellowstone region predictably died from the full spectrum of causes. The majority, about 65% of the 81 deaths logged so far were human-caused — a dynamic that
is the norm
.
Grizzly bear managers break causes of death into nine categories. Higher-than-average causes in 2026 are listed below:
Cattle/livestock depredation: 27 grizzlies (1.6X higher than average)
Accidental: 12 grizzlies (2X average)
Natural: 11 grizzlies (3.6X average)
Illegal poaching: 6 (3X average)
Remaining causes — those that are undetermined, from human-bear site conflicts and “human removal,” self-defense killings or mistaken ID incidents from black bear hunters — either occurred at below average rates this year or haven’t yet been calculated.
Interagency Grizzly Bear Study Team leader Matt Gould told WyoFile in an email that the variations in causes of death occur every year and are “natural and often substantial.”
“Drawing major conclusions from a single year is difficult,” Gould said. “That’s why the long-term monitoring conducted by [the study team] is essential for understanding drivers of population dynamics as they relate to sources of mortality.”
Grizzly bear 399 and her four yearling cubs emerge from their den, walking from Jackson Lake Lodge to Signal Mountain Lodge on April 15, 2021 in Grand Teton National Park. The famous female grizzly died in a vehicle strike three years later. This year at least 10 grizzlies have died in bear-vehicle collisions. (Ryan Dorgan)
Wyoming Game and Fish’s Thompson noted some silver linings to the rough year for grizzly bears. For one, he said, adult female bears — the reproducers and important drivers of the population — fared relatively well, especially in the ecosystem interior zone known as the “demographic monitoring area.”
“Literally one female had been killed in an agency action in the DMA,” Thompson said. “I think that’s relevant because people think we’re just murdering bears left and right, but that’s not necessarily the case.”
Still, it was a rough year overall for grizzlies inside the 19,278-square-mile DMA, which is the
area where wildlife managers estimate
the population. The deadliest year for grizzlies inside that interior zone
took place in 2025
, when 54 known bruins perished. But this year isn’t far off, with 48 mortalities so far.
Another bright spot, according to Thompson: The grizzly bear population has so far absorbed the deaths without declining.
“There’s enough recruitment in the population going on that it compensates for this level of mortality,” he said. “We forget that there are over 300 bears born in the ecosystem every year.”
The now-record counts of grizzly deaths might end up becoming the norm as grizzly densities grow along the ecosystem periphery, Thompson said, though it’s too early to say.
“Maybe this is kind of where we’re at proportionally,” Thompson said. “I still am not worried about the population, whatsoever.”
Former Grizzly Bear Recovery Coordinator Chris Servheen agreed. The nearly 400% occurrence of natural bear-on-bear deaths in the Yellowstone region this year is “interesting,” he said.
“Grizzly bears are self-regulating,” Servheen said. “At higher densities, they tend to have lower subadult survival rates, because males are killing them. Over time, as the population increases in density, the growth of the population will slow because of those adult males and that higher mortality rate among subadults. That’s probably what we’re seeing.”
A grizzly bear killed by hunter gunfire in the Aspen Creek drainage west of Cody in 2014. Grizzly bears have died at a record high rate so far in 2026. Some 17 deaths are being investigated, which means the bears were likely killed in self-defense. (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service)
High mortality numbers alone don’t mean much in regards to the health of the population, he said.
“The key is the trajectory of the population,” Servheen said. “If we saw the trajectory change and start to decline, then I would be really worried about these numbers.”
So far, that’s not the case.
Grizzlies have recovered enough since hitting a low a half-century ago that the ecosystem’s tri-state managers in Wyoming, Montana and Idaho are anticipating a third attempt at “delisting” bears from their threatened status under the Endangered Species Act. In the meantime, the
U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is finalizing a rule
that gives the states more management latitude.
Courts have reversed past attempts to delist grizzlies. In 2007, a ruling rejecting delisting hinged on how the
decline of whitebark pine
— its seeds are a major grizzly food source — would impact the species. In 2017, state jurisdiction was overturned days before the first hunting season in four-plus decades. The court cited
concerns about genetic diversity
and what happens when population numbers are “recalibrated” by scientists changing the way they count bears.
Recent high grizzly bear mortality figures likely won’t factor much into the delisting decision. Nevertheless, it “doesn’t benefit” states’ argument for more control, said Servheen, who’s been a leading voice against delisting. His chief concern is that anti-predator
state politicians influence grizzly bear management
, putting the species at risk.
