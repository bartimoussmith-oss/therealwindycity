Cheyenne gets railway ‘green light’ for Reed Avenue Corridor; project changes coming

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — With the “green light” from the BNSF Railway, the Reed Avenue Rail Corridor Project is closer to fruition than ever before. The Cheyenne Finance Committee, responsible for making financial recommendations to the Cheyenne City Council, heard updates on the long-awaited project and the railway’s agreement to a pedestrian corridor Tuesday.
The project to repurpose Reed Avenue, which follows a small BNSF rail line north from the main tracks near 15th street north through Cheyenne’s West Edge neighborhood, has been in the works for years.
The project would convert the area around the track into a public space fit to serve as a gathering and event spot that celebrates Cheyenne’s rail history. People could walk or bike along the long avenue all the way up from its origins on 15th Street to its end at 24th Street. The rail line would stay, allowing visitors to appreciate Cheyenne’s railroad history up close.
Concept illustration from the 2018 Reed Avenue Rail Corridor Master Plan
However, City Engineer Tom Cobb told the committee Tuesday, some disagreements with BNSF have held up progress. The railway had asked for four street closures where the railroad crossing is level with the street for safety reasons. These would have been enforced at 17th Street, 18th Street, 21st Street and Dillon Avenue where the line goes through.
Because crossings where the line is level with the street pose the greatest risk for car–train accidents, shutting these crossings down was a non-negotiable for the railway. As safety and profit are the railway’s two main priorities, Cobb said, there was no way for the city to negotiate keeping those crossings open for cars.
That impacts businesses like Westby Edge Brewing, whose owners bought their 20th Street building with the rail corridor project and its connectivity to the rest of Cheyenne in mind. Cobb said the 21st Street closure would be particularly bad for business.
The city has worked out a compromise, though. In exchange for closing the four crossings, BNSF will dedicate an 8-foot pedestrian corridor on the opposite side of the tracks from the original 34-foot pedestrian promenade to sustain some connectivity, keep people flowing on the west side of the tracks and keep them from jumping the tracks themselves.
“It was a compromise. Both sides lost a little bit,” Cobb said. “We lost the closure on 21st Street, but they agreed to lose a bit of their maintenance road to account for the additional pedestrians because the point was made to them that people are going to try to get over the path no matter what we do.”
The railway’s attitude toward the project in its entirety seems to have improved, Cobb added. In a July meeting with railway executives, Cobb and Cheyenne Mayor Patrick Collins were reportedly told that the BNSF wanted to make this a “national demonstration project” showing what good can come from the railway partnering with communities.
“In my mind, it was absolutely huge,” Cobb said. “As you all know, and I’ve reported this to all of you before, it has moved so slow in trying to navigate this process, but I felt like at that particular juncture, the floodgates have now opened for them. They don’t only want it, they want to use it as a demonstration project — a completely different story.”
With the closures and the addition of the new pedestrian corridor, the scope of the project is expected to change significantly. Most notably — and why it was the Cheyenne Finance Committee hearing from Cobb — is that the city will need to agree to an $1,166,647 increase in the contract total for the project, making it cost $2,487,566 in full.
That will require an extra $466,620 from reserve Fifth Penny funds, $422,527 in Sixth Penny funds and, with the help of BNSF, $277,500 from a Federal Railroad Administration grant.
The grant specifically addresses the cost of eliminating the railway street crossings, as well as some noise studies and environmental compliance work required for the project. During meetings with Cobb and Collins, BNSF reportedly agreed to assist with the grant application process, helping the city navigate the FRA grant process and reporting and compliance requirements.
Because 17th, 18th and 21st streets are now essentially closed to vehicle traffic, the city also has more it can do with those streets between Snyder and Bent Avenues, the two streets running parallel to either side of Reed Avenue. A complete reconstruction of those areas will now occur, Cobb confirmed, including landscaping, sidewalk grading and site aesthetics to match the improvements on Reed. A nearby parking lot between 20th and 21st streets was also added.
With more steam than ever before, major improvements are on their way to one of Cheyenne’s oldest neighborhoods, much to the satisfaction of businesses like Westby Edge.
“It goes without saying that I’m in 100% support of this,” Brennan Westby, the business’s brewmaster, told the committee. “I think it’s the main reason why we bought the building that we did. It’s not because the building was perfect; as we all know, it really wasn’t. Seeing the steam and motivation that’s going into this right now especially, I just want to see that keep going and see this project succeed.”
The project additions will push the completion date back. According to city documents, the project is now expected to be completed in spring 2028. The project is also entirely contingent on the support of the Cheyenne City Council. Earning a 2–1 recommendation from the Finance Committee Tuesday, it will advance to the council for final consideration with the committee’s support Monday, Nov. 9. Councilor Mark Moody was the single “no” vote.
