What do election results tell us about Wyoming politics?
Nov 21, 2026

Even if it happened as the polls predicted, no one expected the “blue wave” to inundate deep-red Wyoming.
That certainly held true Nov. 3, as Equality State voters once again filled the state’s top five executive branch positions and all three of the state’s seats in Congress with Republicans. In addition, all but one seat in the state Legislature stayed in the GOP’s control.
But even as the outcome was nearly identical to previous cycles, the way it happened was different, some of the newcomers are very different from their predecessors, and it offers some lessons — and leads to some interesting questions — for the future.
Voter turnout, willingness to pay for services
Before we get into the details of who won and by what margins, we were pleased to see voter participation numbers top the 2022 midterm election (68% in Laramie County this year, compared with 63% four years ago). Those numbers still could be higher, of course, but it’s good to see so many people engaged and committed to making their voices heard.
People obviously vote for different reasons — and many will vote no matter who or what is on the ballot — but we can’t help but wonder if statewide Proposition 1 was a factor in the larger voter turnout this year. According to unofficial results from the Secretary of State’s Office, 185,231 people voted either for or against the proposal to cut residential property tax assessments by 50%.
The citizen-led measure failed by a margin of 58% to 42% both statewide and here in Laramie County after a widespread campaign to make residents aware of the local impact on vital services. At the same time, Laramie County voters renewed the fifth-penny sales tax, and, during the Aug. 18 primary election, supported 13 of the 14 sixth-penny sales tax propositions.
This tells us that even though they may want their tax bills to be lower — really, who doesn’t? — most residents understand they need to pay for the local services they count on every day.
Shift to the left — trend or one-off?
Leaders of the Democratic Party in Wyoming say they’re encouraged by the fact that the two Republicans elected to Congress won by narrower margins than in the past.
In their minds, the fact that all counties in the state voted more Democratic this year points to an opportunity to gradually turn the Cowboy State from red to purple, then blue.
We think that’s unlikely, for several reasons:
Wyoming voters have consistently been more conservative than the rest of the country, and we don’t see that changing anytime soon. In fact, we’ve had many newcomers tell us they’ve moved here in recent years because places like Texas weren’t conservative enough for them.
Most Wyoming residents tend to be more pragmatic than partisan. That means they’re likely to vote for the candidate they think will best represent them and their priorities, rather than a straight party-line ticket. While it’s true voters have elected Democrats as governor in the past, they haven’t done so since Dave Freudenthal won reelection to a second term in 2006, and Wyoming hasn’t voted to send a Democrat to Washington, D.C., since 1972. At the same time, several of the candidates and issues endorsed by state Republican Party leaders failed this year.
All 23 counties saw more voters cast ballots for Democrats in the U.S. Senate race than in the 2024 presidential election, and the Republican candidate won the U.S. House seat by the slimmest margin (54.2%) of any Republican since 2008. But we believe that has more to do with the quality of the candidates this year on both sides than a decision to support the Democratic Party.
Time for a third party? GOP needs to analyze results
Having a candidate for statewide office earn more than 10% of the vote earned “major party” status for the Constitution Party. The question is whether it will play a major role in future elections.
Will more members of the Republican Party run under the Constitution Party banner if they disagree with the GOP leadership’s position on issues or fail to gain their support? Will that siphon enough votes to give Democrats an advantage in certain races?
This potential should cause leaders of the Wyoming Republican Party to analyze the results from both Aug. 18 and Nov. 3. Their support of Proposition 1 also shows they need to get out and talk with average residents to find out what’s most important to them.
Voters reject extremism, political infighting
The strong message from the August primary was that Wyoming voters are tired of the extremism and infighting within the GOP. They largely rejected the Freedom Caucus’ approach in the Legislature and supported candidates who promised to get things done for the good of the entire state.
The first test of whether this reset was successful starts Jan. 12, when members of the 69th Wyoming Legislature take their seats at the state Capitol. The culture and results of the ensuing 40-day session will indicate whether the state’s political culture is returning to the values and priorities that have shaped the state since it was founded 136 years ago.
We hope lawmakers will dig in and work hard on the issues impacting the state’s residents every day — housing affordability, healthcare access and insurance costs, good-paying jobs, keeping young people in the state, etc. — rather than continuing to pass bills that are solutions looking for problems.
To some, this year’s election results signal a shift to the left. To us, they indicate an urge to refocus on what we value most about Wyoming — we take care of our neighbors, we can disagree with one another respectfully, and we don’t answer to anyone outside our borders.
Time will tell if it holds, but we hope the 2026 elections sent a strong message that the power rests with the people, and they should always be at the center of decisions made by elected officials at all levels.
Wyoming Tribune Eagle
Nov. 14
