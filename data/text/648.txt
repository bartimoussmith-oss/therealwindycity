Wyoming Chamber of Commerce executive: Data centers bring jobs, but state should set expectations

RAWLINS — Data centers are bringing investments and jobs to Cheyenne, but the state has not set clear enough expectations for the industry, according to a Wyoming Chamber of Commerce executive.
Cassie Hoover, vice president of growth and development for the Wyoming Chamber of Commerce and the Greater Cheyenne Chamber of Commerce, spoke at a business policy briefing at the old train depot on Nov. 17.
Hoover said the chamber supports the projects as free enterprise and a boost to national security, while acknowledging concerns about water, workforce and public opinion.
She estimated Cheyenne has 12 to 15 data centers. WyoFile reported in May that the city has 13 large ones, including a National Center for Atmospheric Research supercomputer. Microsoft opened its first Cheyenne data center in 2012, and Meta has a campus under construction. On Nov. 5, Google announced its first Wyoming data center, a $17 billion investment in Laramie County being developed with Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy, American Electric Power and Related Digital. Google said the project will support thousands of jobs and produce hundreds of millions of dollars in local property taxes.
The chamber’s support rests on economic and security arguments, Hoover said.
“We, as the chamber, we support free enterprise, so we support data centers,” she said. “They bring economic development to our state and bring in money, and above all of that, they in- crease our national security. And if we want to stay ahead of China, we gotta do these data centers.”
Regarding the creation of jobs, Hoover said most projects are in the construction phase.
"They, of course, in the construction phases, bring in more jobs than they do like in their permanent phase, and so that will decrease after," she said.
She said staffing is a challenge.
"One of the biggest issues that comes with these big projects is we don't have enough people to feed into the workforce, which creates issues because then we have to do workforce housing and bring people in," she said.
An attendee said Rawlins faces a similar problem, with hotels often the only place to house incoming workers.
Hoover said Cheyenne is requiring data centers to have water recycling systems.
"From what I've heard from Google and Meta and Microsoft, which are the larger ones, they don't use that much more water than Black Hills Energy or the other large corporations that we have," she said.
Public reaction has been mixed, Hoover said.
A chamber luncheon with data center and utility representatives drew about 300 people, and "we had a huge wait list," she said.
"I would say it started off really, really rough, and it's progressively gotten better,” Hoover said. “But we still have a group that doesn't like data centers. Can't keep everyone happy, I guess."
Opposition has reached state politics.
WyoFile reported that Secretary of State Chuck Gray has come out against new AI data centers in Wyoming.
Hoover said the state needs to do more up front.
"Wyoming hasn't prepared enough for kind of lining out the expectations for those data centers, and we should be learning from places like Virginia," she said.
She also said local officials should set expectations earlier.
"The city has to do a little bit better job on setting those expectations prior, instead of getting in like a survival mode type situation," she said.
She added that a remediation plan would help, and the chamber intends to form a political action committee to address those concerns.
Hoover said she saw Meta donate $10,000 to the Boys and Girls Club in Cheyenne. She encouraged rural business owners to pursue company grants.
"I would motivate you guys to reach out to the data centers because a lot of them have specific projects that are for rural communities," she said.
