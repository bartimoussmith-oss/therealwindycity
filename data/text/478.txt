Pickles are everywhere, including trending cocktails
Mandy V Applegate

Two jars can share the same kosher dill label and still taste noticeably different. The brine changes a pickle’s taste, from bright green half-sour to sweet bread-and-butter. The salty liquid that turns a cucumber into a pickle also shows up in other places, including tasty cocktails.
Photo credit: Depositphotos.
A recent
food and beverage forecast
found that 1 in 85 U.S. food and beverage consumers now reach for something preserved, up from 1 in 95 a year ago, and that talk of preservation methods is growing while words such as “ancient” and “sophisticated” are falling. The measure covers preserved foods and drinks broadly, not pickles alone. For pickles, that makes the brine the part of the label worth reading.
National Pickle Day on Nov. 14 is a good excuse to sort the jars by brine. The sections below start with the main styles of cucumber pickle, then move from the jar to cocktails and a limited-edition beer. Home canning and refrigerator methods come next, and pickled vegetables other than cucumbers get a section near the end.
The type of brine decides which kind of pickle ends up in the jar
A half-sour spends only a week or two in a
salt brine
and leaves before it fully ferments, keeping it bright green and crisp with a subtler taste. Left in the brine for about three months, the same cucumber becomes a full-sour that is darker and more intense. Brine time is the main difference, so the two styles show how much of a pickle’s character comes from the process, not the cucumber.
Traditional kosher dills sit in a salt brine flavored with garlic and dill, and the name refers to that New York Jewish style rather than to dietary law. Dill is what separates them from half-sours and full-sours, which
leave it out
. Many mass-market jars labeled kosher dills use a vinegar brine instead, which gives them a different flavor than the salted-water originals. That makes the ingredient list, and not the name, the place to tell the two apart.
Cornichons
are the French version of the small pickle that English speakers call a gherkin. Makers soak cornichons, picked at 1 to 2 inches, in vinegar, cook them and then jar them with herbs. They have a mild tang and a crisp texture, and they taste much like dill pickles but less tart.
For bread-and-butter pickles, add
brown sugar or sugar syrup
to the brine and generally leave out the garlic that gives kosher pickles their flavor. The sugar goes into the brine itself, so sweetness is part of the method and not a flavor added later. A bread-and-butter jar and a dill jar can start with identical cucumbers and still taste very different.
Pickle brine and pickle flavor turn up in martinis and beer
A gin-based pickle martini pairs a mist of vermouth and a dash of celery bitters with pickle juice, then finishes with extra pickles as a garnish. Smirnoff’s Pickletini takes the vodka route, shaking 1 1/2 ounces of vodka with 1/4 ounce of vermouth and a measure of pickle juice, then straining it into a martini glass and garnishing with a pickle.
Both drinks use the jar twice, once for the juice and once for the garnish, which makes either one an easy fit for gatherings. The same jar also goes into plenty of
recipes
, including a
fried dill pickle dip
that calls for chopped dill pickles and pickle juice.
Pabst Blue Ribbon and Grillo’s Pickles released a
limited-edition pickle beer
in May at 4.7% ABV. The beer is billed as bright and tangy, with a crisp, dill-forward character that balances the lager’s maltiness. The release ties the beer to the dive-bar ritual of dropping a pickle spear into a beer.
Home cooks have 4 ways to pickle a cucumber
Home cooks can
ferment pickles
,
process them in a
canner
, freeze them or keep them in the refrigerator.
Refrigerator pickles
skip the canning step, so they aren’t shelf stable and need to stay cold; the jars still need sterilizing before filling. The method also suits produce that has no research-based canning recipe. Labels can mislead, since recipes called quick pickled can turn out to be refrigerator pickles, while fresh-pack pickles go through a canner and stay shelf stable.
Crispness depends as much on the cucumber as on the method, and in-season produce gives the best flavor and crispness. Cooks should skip wax-coated cucumbers because wax can interfere with acidification.
Dry brining
with pickling salt can improve crispness, and
calcium chloride products
are an optional way to firm the pickles.
Plenty of other vegetables go into the pickle jar
The pickle jar holds plenty of produce besides cucumbers, including asparagus, beets, carrots and snap beans. Peppers, green tomatoes, cabbage and summer squash also fall among the
vegetables suited to pickling
. Relishes and chutneys also count as pickled products. Cooks trim dilly beans from 5 to 6 inches long to 4-inch lengths and let them stand at least two weeks before tasting. Home cooks who would rather skip the canner can keep a jar of
pickled yellow beans
in the refrigerator.
The word
gherkin
names a separate plant species, “Cucumis anguria,” a trailing vine in the gourd family. Its furrowed, prickly fruits can be eaten raw, cooked or pickled. The gherkins in commercial pickle mixes are usually small, immature common cucumbers instead.
2 jars with different brines make an easy taste test
The easiest way to test the differences on National Pickle Day is to open two jars labeled with different brines. Tasting a slice from each jar back to back puts the crunch and tang side by side. Whichever one wins the comparison is the style worth buying again.
