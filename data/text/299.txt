Judge upholds $1.5M bond as UW grad student coffee poisoning case heads to District Court

INSET: Aili Wang, 32. OUTSET: The University of Wyoming Agriculture Building (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
LARAMIE, Wyo. — An Albany County Circuit Court judge found probable cause Tuesday to send the
attempted murder case of University of Wyoming graduate student Aili Wang
to District Court.
Judge Robert Sanford also denied a defense request to lower Wang’s $1.5 million bond.
Wang, who appeared by video during the 10:30 a.m. preliminary hearing, faces felony charges of attempted second-degree murder and aggravated assault and battery. The charges follow the
suspected poisoning of a coworker
in her research lab.
Sanford bound the case over after University of Wyoming Police Chief Josh Holland testified about the department’s investigation.
Federal law enforcement took Wang into custody at Los Angeles International Airport in October on suspicion of trying to flee the country.
She traveled to California shortly after an Oct. 5 interview with campus police
. Officers had opened an investigation after a fellow graduate student reported falling violently ill twice after drinking tea made with water from an office coffee pot.
According to Holland’s testimony and court documents, both Wang and the student were studying molecular biology
. They shared an office in the university’s Agriculture Building and worked in the same lab group
. Holland testified that their working relationship had grown cold.
The student reported tasting a bitter flavor in his tea on Aug. 10 and Aug. 17
. Shortly after drinking it, he suffered numbness, balance issues and severe liver damage.
Suspicious of foul play, the student and his wife, a chemistry postgraduate student at the university, gathered a 100-milliliter water sample from the coffee pot
. They boiled it down, leaving behind 63 milligrams of a white powder
. A field test confirmed the powder contained acrylamide
.
Acrylamide is an industrial chemical and neurotoxin found in trace amounts in some foods
. High doses can cause nerve damage and organ failure.
The couple notified campus police on Sept. 18. Investigators installed a hidden camera facing the student’s desk. On Oct. 1, the camera recorded Wang handling the coffee maker, pouring water between the student’s mug and her own, and taking pictures of documents on his desk.
During a police interview a few days later, Wang denied handling or buying acrylamide in recent years. A search of her email records, however, revealed two orders totaling 500 grams of the chemical delivered to her in July and September.
The following day, Wang purchased a plane ticket to China. Law enforcement stopped her at the airport before her flight.
During Tuesday’s hearing, Holland said investigators consulted a former State Crime Lab toxicologist, who told him that with acrylamide, “the dose would be the poison.” The toxicologist referenced a Japan case study where a woman died after drinking 150 milliliters of an acrylamide solution. The UW student drank about 20 milliliters, Holland said.
Prosecutors argued that the purchases, surveillance footage, lab results and physical symptoms were enough to establish sufficient probable cause to move the case forward.
Wang’s defense attorney argued the state relied entirely on circumstantial evidence. Counsel said the surveillance footage showed Wang touching items on the desk, but never captured her putting anything into the coffee machine’s reservoir.
The defense also challenged the testing methods. Holland confirmed that the initial test did not determine how much pure acrylamide made up the 63 milligrams of residue, and complete laboratory results remain pending. Holland also agreed under cross-examination that standard hospital tests cannot detect acrylamide in the human body.
Defense counsel suggested the complainant’s symptoms could stem from academic stress related to his dissertation
. Counsel added that the two students researched different subjects, leaving Wang with no reason to disrupt his work
. Holland testified, however, that investigators believe Wang previously worked for a Chinese firm conducting similar research
.
In seeking a bond reduction, the defense argued that $1.5 million acted as detention, arguing that Wang had surrendered her passport and had ties to the area. Prosecutors argued against a lower bond, arguing that Wang had a $3,000 gold bar when arrested and maintained access to foreign money.
Sanford kept bond set at $1.5 million and ordered Wang bound over to District Court for arraignment
. A district court arraignment date has not yet been set
.
Wang is presumed innocent until proven guilty in a court of law.
