Wyoming teams with Trump admin to expedite energy, industrial projects
Amid criticism in Wyoming for some accelerated industrial project reviews, Gov. Mark Gordon says the partnership should ensure more transparency.
by
Wyofile
October 28, 2026
October 28, 2026

Gov. Mark Gordon and Federal Permitting Improvement Steering Council Executive Director Emily Domenech sign a memorandum of understanding on Oct. 26, 2026 "to prevent unnecessarily long reviews for critical infrastructure projects." (Governor Mark Gordon's office)
by
Dustin Bleizeffer
,
WyoFile
Wyoming will partner with the Trump administration on an initiative to streamline and expedite permitting for large energy and other industrial projects such as data centers, representatives from both groups announced Monday.
Gov. Mark Gordon joined Federal Permitting Improvement Steering Council Executive Director Emily Domenech during a press conference Monday afternoon to sign a
memorandum of understanding
“to prevent unnecessarily long reviews for critical infrastructure projects.” The agreement promises to align Wyoming with the administration’s expedited permitting efforts, which have included shortened environmental reviews and public commenting periods.
“I’m really excited because I think the relationship that we’re going to put together today will prove mutually beneficial, because it will help make sure that our environmental reviews [and] federal environmental reviews are done expeditiously,” Gordon said.
Some federally expedited environmental reviews in Wyoming, however, have already caused heartache among residents and local officials, like the
Seminoe pumped water storage hydroelectric project
that has raised concerns about a prized trout fishery downstream and a bighorn sheep herd.
But the governor noted that some large energy projects in the state have endured years — and more than a decade, in some cases — of permitting reviews before final approval. The process can take so long that markets often change the economic viability and scope of a project.
From left to right, Siemens Energy North America President Rich Voorberg, Utah Office of Energy Director Gregory Todd, Colorado Energy Office Advisor James Lester, Interior Secretary Deb Haaland, Gov. Mark Gordon and Nevada Office of Energy Director Dwyane McClinton participate in a groundbreaking ceremony June 20, 2023 for the TransWest Express transmission line in Carbon County. (Dustin Bleizeffer/WyoFile)
“If it takes us eight years just to get through scoping [the initial federal, state and public reviews], and things can change as dramatically as they have, then it really slows things down,” he said.
The
Federal Permitting Improvement Steering Council
, created in President Barack Obama’s second term, has been instrumental in implementing the Trump administration’s efforts to accelerate permitting reviews for energy and other large projects such as artificial intelligence computing centers. The agency, Domenech said, expedites permitting timelines while providing more transparency, including via its
permitting dashboard
— a tool that will now include information about Wyoming’s permitting agency actions on particular projects.
“We can help to make the environmental permitting process more transparent, more predictable and more accessible to developers who are trying to develop the incredible natural resources and grow the economies of the states across the country,” Domenech said. “This agreement we’re signing today is a cornerstone of what I think the president has really brought to every governor in the country, which is, ‘We want to help you grow your economy, create more jobs, bring more industry to your state.’”
The agency has helped the Trump administration reduce the average time it took the federal permitting review process from 1,500 days to 400, Domenech added.
“I want to stress we’ve done that by following the law,” she said. “We’re meeting every single standard that’s set in the law. We’re finishing reviews on time and we’re making sure projects can move forward faster.”
Permitting heartache
Prospective developers, including many Wyoming-based companies planning to serve them, are not the only ones with heartache over the permitting process.
Gov. Mark Gordon, Interior Secretary Deb Haaland and Department of Energy Secretary Jennifer Granholm speak at a TransWest Express groundbreaking celebration June 20, 2023 in Carbon County. The project took some 15 years to permit and gain approval from landowners. (Dustin Bleizeffer/WyoFile)
Many industrial projects in the state have raised the ire of local communities that say they either don’t have enough time to keep up with the permitting process or that their efforts to tailor fit a project gets a cold response.
The
Seminoe pumped water storage
hydroelectric project in Carbon County spurred residents and local officials alike to plead for more time and local input, for example. Residents in Crook County say they were caught off guard by the federally expedited
Bear Lodge rare earth mining project
. Both projects were granted FAST-41 status — an expedited federal permitting review.
Asked by WyoFile how Wyoming’s cooperation with the federal permitting council might address those concerns, Gordon said, “The MOU will allow for better coordination with the state to make sure that we really do give those communities an opportunity to participate in the process.
“Being a FAST-41 project doesn’t guarantee you get a permit,” Gordon added. “There’s nothing in the law that says you get it. You still have to do all of the things that are required in order to finish the permitting process. But what it does require is that we have a transparent forum for anyone in the community to go and see that work.”
Domenech has made rounds in the national media recently to bolster support for a bipartisan
bill now before Congress
. It would galvanize the Trump administration’s efforts to speed up permitting, in part, by limiting state authority and litigation powers over approved projects,
she told Bloomberg this month
.
“So how do we stop frivolous lawsuits from taking down projects and how do we reform the Clean Water Act so we don’t have a system where states can really veto any project?” Domenech told Bloomberg. “Those things have to stay in this bill in order for it to get to a place where it gets to the president’s desk and he’s ready to sign it.”
Is that in Wyoming’s best interest, WyoFile asked the governor.
“If all you want to do is stop stuff,” Gordon responded, “then [courts are a] really a great process. But on the other hand, you want to make sure things are done correctly, according to law and done in a timely fashion, we do need to have some permitting reform.”
Domenech said her agency, empowered by the Trump administration and the potential of further Congressional permitting reform, should provide a cleaner process to ensure projects don’t face court challenges.
“When we think about the best way for permitting to move forward, it’s more work on the front end to be more transparent and better coordinated across agencies so that you can have confidence in your permit going forward,” Domenech told WyoFile. “That’s the core of the permitting certainty argument, is you do your work right the first time and then it should be harder to sue that project.”
