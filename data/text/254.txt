SHERIDAN — Pharmacies of Sheridan’s past weren’t just drugstores.
In the early 1900s, customers could browse stationery and share a cigar, pull up a chair for ice cream soda and visit with neighbors while druggists prepared remedies behind the counter.
Kathleen Laya and Valerie Spanos, daughters of longtime Sheridan pharmacist Bill Laya, said drugstores at the turn of the 20th century were social hubs with professionals who didn’t rely on documented forms or premeasured compounds to make medicine. These druggists would use raw ingredients to make their own elixirs in-house, offering patients herbal medicines like henna leaves, wormseed and aluminum hydroxide.
These medicines, Laya said, are precursors to modern-day forms of over-the-counter medicine. It was all during an era that predated modern prescriptions and standardized prescriptions.
“(Doctors) didn’t have nice forms,” Spanos said. “They had the back of menus from The Sheridan Inn, and they had envelopes and just any scrap of paper a doctor could find (to) write the prescription on.”
For Spanos, a Buffalo-based historian, and Laya, a biopharmaceutical executive, interest in Sheridan’s pharmacy history is rooted in family connection. When their father purchased a downtown Sheridan pharmacy in 1963, he obtained its business book, customer records and inventory. He stored and preserved nearly 100 years of this history in storage boxes and containers.
A century and a half later, thousands of these records are still preserved in cigar boxes in a storage unit off North Main Street, where Laya and Spanos have been carefully organizing Sheridan’s pharmaceutical history that stems back to the 1880s. They believe this collection — passed down to them after their parents’ passing — offers a rare glimpse into generations of Sheridan’s medical history.
Laya and Spanos are using scanning technology and artificial intelligence to protect this history, while creating an online platform through Brannian Auction to sell other items of the family’s extensive Old West antique and pharmacy collection.
Historical discoveries
Today, Hospital Pharmacy Prescriptions, Cards and Gifts is the lone drugstore left standing in Sheridan’s downtown sector. But for decades, it wasn’t the only one.
Records from The Wyoming Room show pharmacies in Sheridan dating back to the 1880s. Edelman Drugs, at the corner of Main Street and Loucks, and Northern Drug, in the current home of Brian’s Boot & Shoe Co., were fixtures of the small Western community. Throughout the years, they were joined by Brown’s Drugstore and Soda Fountain and Tyson’s Drug Store.
Spanos and Laya’s parents, William and Joyce Laya, also have a long history in Sheridan. William was born in 1930 in Monarch. After serving as a medic in the 300th field artillery during the Korean War, he returned to Sheridan and became a pharmacist, purchasing Northern Drug Store in 1963, and operating several other drugstores during this time. He met Joyce while she was working as a nurse at Sheridan Memorial Hospital.
Medicine also runs in the family’s blood. Of her five siblings, Laya said one became a physician, another an optometrist and another an audiologist.
Spanos and Laya’s parents instilled a passion for history in their children.
“Together, they just became kind of collectors of all different paraphernalia,” Spanos said.
Spanos remembers her brother crawling underneath the crawlspace of the Northern Drug building and discovering the antique bottles their parents had acquired from the pharmacy sale. But it was her mother who wanted her children to document the collection’s history. She died in 2019.
“She is the one who had the vision,” Spanos said. “She said, ‘Nobody’s done a history of all the doctors that have worked in Sheridan over the years, and that’s how you can verify who they were and where they worked.’”
Spanos began scanning the prescription collection in September in the back storage area of Money Lenders at 555 N. Main St. In this collection, Spanos believes she’s found prescriptions for members of Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Shows and tracked the careers of Sheridan’s earliest doctors. Among these are Dr. Elizabeth McNutt Simpson, a female doctor who worked in Sheridan in 1901, according to Spanos’ findings.
“It’s really unique because it’s an intact pharmacy that goes from the late 19th (century) up to the 1960s, and potentially beyond,” Laya said.
Some of the most dated writings in the collection are still legible and easy to read, and Laya credits this to Wyoming’s dry climate, and the cool, dark places the family has stored these artifacts in over the years. Laya said the family has kept the collection in the same storage unit since the 1990s.
These records make up more than a dozen boxes of the collection, but Laya said they will not be available for auction, as the two sisters work together to scan and document the history online. But Laya said the auction will include some of the pharmacies’ original apothecary tables with their labels intact, along with trinkets from the early 1900s.
Integrating AI
Spanos said scanning what she estimates to be hundreds of thousands of prescriptions felt daunting when she first began. But using AI technology has made that load more manageable.
Spanos uses AI to analyze the pages Spanos scans into a computer and create a catalog. She monitors the algorithm to ensure it is reading the freehand writing correctly.
“Before we get started, right before we really get into this, (we’re trying to think), “What is the best system to use?” Laya said. "How do we protect ourselves while still getting what we want out of it?”
Laya said the family will also monitor HIPAA guidelines. A person’s private health information is protected for 50 years after their death, according to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. This means a young child who was prescribed cough medicine in 1930, but died in 1990, would still be protected under HIPPA and therefore not scanned for research purposes.
The duo are also searching for potential funding avenues or part-time help to take this project to the next level. They hope to create a large database and research the people they find listed in the records. The project takes time and commitment, but Laya and Spanos said it has significant potential to add depth to the region’s current medical documentation.
“Our biggest challenge right now is time,” Laya said. “We’re looking at, once we get a system set up, if there is an opportunity to maybe get a grant or use students… to help with the scanning and uploading.”
Spanos said she’s most interested to learn more about the history of Sheridan’s medical professionals, and what medicines they continued prescribing throughout the 20th century.
A storied collection
As the Laya family continues to document Sheridan’s earliest prescriptions, they part with other pieces of Sheridan’s history their family has held onto for 50 years.
Their upcoming auction includes antiques from Sheridan’s oldest pharmacies — but it also showcases other well-known periods of Sheridan’s history, including maps and artifacts from the Monarch coal mines of the early 1900s, Depression-era dresses and campaign posters from previous Wyoming political races. Cash registers, safes and church pews from the early 1900s also provide glimpses into Sheridan County’s early history.
While both Laya and Spanos said putting some of these items up for bid was an emotional decision, they feel hopeful that many of the pieces can go to people who want to appreciate the history behind them.
“My ideal person would come in here and go, ‘We’re going to put this in a museum,” Spanos said.
“Or somebody who was going to study it (and) use it,” Laya added.
The collection is available for viewing and bidding online from now until 6 p.m. Nov. 5 through
Brannian Auction
, with an in-person viewing at 555 N. Main St. 4:30-6 p.m. Nov. 4.
