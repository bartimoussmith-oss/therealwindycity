How Lula turned the tables on the right in Brazil and the US with his comeback presidential victory
Anthony W. Pereira, Tulane University; King's College London
Oct 26, 2026
4 hrs ago

A leader who stood up to U.S. President Donald Trump just won an election in Latin America’s biggest country, reversing a
trend of new right-wing governments
in the region over the past two years.
Incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva,
having narrowly lost
to Flávio Bolsonaro in the first round of voting on Oct. 5, 2026, staged a remarkable comeback on Oct. 25. He erased the deficit and won the
all-important runoff election
by more than 3.5 percentage points – more than 4 million votes.
It was a stunning result for the leader who proved to be the first Brazilian candidate to win a presidential election after not winning the first round, and who had been all but written off for a possible win. As a
professor of Latin American politics
, I believe the result offers a number of key political lessons for Brazil, Latin America – and even the United States.
A contest of worldviews — and social media
The choice in the second-round election was stark. Lula offered measures to ameliorate steep income inequalities and an economy built on a transition to clean energy, environmental protection and increased access to higher education and technical training. On international matters, Lula balanced defending Brazilian sovereignty from a Trump administration
keen on meddling in Latin America
, and maintaining good relations with an array of nations and blocs, including the European Union and China.
Bolsonaro, meanwhile, promised sweeping cuts to government spending, more militarized policing and eager support for the Trump administration’s
campaign against drug violence
. His pitch also came with offers to U.S. investors
of access to critical minerals
in Brazil.
Such right-wing populism has been ascendant in Latin America of late, and Lula also had to overcome an anti-incumbent mood, brought on by high prices, stagnant salaries, steep interest rates and a
massive corruption scandal
involving Brazilian elites. That banking probe directly
ensnared Bolsonaro himself
, as well as powerful Supreme Court justice Alexandre de Moraes, whom Bolsonaro’s supporters
had tried to link
to Lula.
Lula also had to neuter a social media landscape that has been
dominated by images
and
narratives
disparaging the president and his Workers’ Party.
Right-wing
dominance of that online ecosystem
helped carry
Jair Bolsonaro, the 2026 hopeful’s father, to power in 2018 and got him close to reelection in 2022.
Especially after the first-round results in the 2026 vote, many analysts assumed the same
pro-Bolsonaro social media presence
would once again be hegemonic.
Brazilian lawmaker André Janones embraces President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva as he delivers a victory speech after winning a fourth term in the presidential runoff on Oct. 25, 2026.
AP Photo/Andre Penner via The Conversation
Instead, after the first round, a network of young, tech-savvy pro-Lula communicators created and shared effective, often amusing videos on WhatsApp and Instagram.
And they appealed to the center and even some of the right. All told, Lula easily made up more than 2 million votes by which he lost in the first round. He didn’t just increase turnout by millions and lower abstention rates; he also saw the state with the country’s second-largest electorate, Minas Gerais,
flip to his side entirely
– a swing of nearly 700,000 votes.
Emblematic of the strength of the campaign was the growth in subscribers to the
WhatsApp channel Lula’s Army
, which grew from 50,000 on Oct. 5 to more than 10 million by the night of the historic election.
The creator of the channel, André Janones, is a federal deputy for the Rede Party, an ally of the Workers’ Party, and a rising star in the circle around Lula. Likewise, Ana Elisa Santos Silva, a 21-year-old
digital influencer and law student
in the Workers’ Party, got the most votes of any federal deputy in the swing state of Minas Gerais.
Cracks in the ‘Americanization’ theory of politics
A prevalent narrative of Brazilian politics has held that the country’s divisions increasingly mirror those of the United States.
Part of that idea is due to Trump’s loss in the 2020 election and the resulting attack on the U.S. Capitol on Jan. 6, 2021, which
eerily presaged events
following Jair Bolsanaro’s 2022 loss.
Like in the U.S., there has also been a rapid rise of a
politically active evangelical right
in Brazil.
On Sept. 7, 2026, supporters of Flávio Bolsonaro march with an effigy of U.S. President Donald Trump holding a doll depicting Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva as a prisoner.
AP Photo/Andre Penner via The Conversation
More fundamentally, proponents of the
Americanization thesis
hold that U.S.-style deindustrialization and “Uberization” of the Brazilian economy have led to a decline of the unionized working class – a core support base for the left – and its replacement by an army of gig employees
dreaming of owning their own businesses
and throwing off the shackles of the taxing and regulating state.
Yet similarities between the two countries shouldn’t be confused for politics always moving on parallel tracks.
Survey
research suggests
that many Brazilians see the
state as responsible
for the realization of their constitutionally protected social rights, including healthcare, education and pensions. Many also regard government employment as inherently more stable and dignified than the harsh labor markets of the private sector, especially for the relatively unskilled.
For all of the apparent rise in American-style libertarian politics in Brazil, the reality is that such a viewpoint remains much smaller in the country. Flávio Bolsonaro’s bet, like his father’s before him, was that this strain of libertarian right-wing politics could nonetheless propel him to victory,
much as it apparently had for Javier Milei
in Argentina in 2023. But it was not enough.
One election can’t fix deep polarization
Even with Lula’s win, Flávio Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party did exceptionally well at the legislative level.
When the next Brazilian Congress convenes on Feb. 1, 2027, the
Liberal Party will control
28 seats in the Senate – 35% of the total; and 121 seats in the lower house – 24% of the total (as well as five governorships, which are 19% of the total).
When factoring in expected Liberal Party allies, it is clear that the right will have bigger majorities in both houses for Lula’s next administration. That will help
Congress solidify
control vis-a-vis the executive branch in terms of legislation and, crucially, the budget.
A series of unknowns facing Lula
The election raises several questions.
First, it is unclear how Lula, who will turn 81 on Oct. 27, will be able to effectively govern. The president will have to make major concessions to a Congress with little capacity for responsibly delivering coherent policy.
The federal Supreme Court is also seen as politicized, fragmented and tainted by scandals. On the other hand, Lula
could appoint
four new Supreme Court justices because of upcoming retirements, meaning that he could shape the country’s highest judicial body in a way that enhances his ability to manage Congress.
Yet it might be difficult for Lula to simultaneously meet the demands coming from Congress and those from the business community. For example, many will push the government to curb the fiscal deficit, currently running at a
very high annual level
of close to 8% of gross domestic product.
A second question is whether Bolsonaro’s loss will splinter the right. One interpretation of the 2026 election is that to be competitive, the right will want to move on from the Bolsonaro family. One potential figure is Tarcísio de Freitas, a former cabinet member in the Jair Bolsonaro government and popular governor of São Paulo. Yet there are nonetheless five members of the Bolsonaro family in the new Congress, and they are likely to try to keep together the coalition that fought for Flávio Bolsonaro’s campaign.
Finally, it’s not clear how the Trump administration, which
interfered decisively against Lula’s agenda
, will react to his win – and to what extent it will support Bolsonaro’s denial of his election loss even though it was bigger than his father’s in 2022.
Whatever the extent of any interference, the Trump administration’s right-wing Latin American vision invariably sets up conflict with Lula. Yet while the Trump preference was always for Flávio Bolsonaro, that doesn’t mean his administration won’t pragmatically engage with Lula
even as it pressures
his government. For his part, Lula continues to hold his own leverage, including through Brazil’s
large deposits of rare earth elements
that the Trump administration covets.
If the past eight years of Brazilian politics are any guide, Lula’s next administration is likely to be a turbulent one in which enduring
divisions at home and abroad
are continually tested.
