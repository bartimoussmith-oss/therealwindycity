From the sidelines to the Capitol: Harshman prepares for role as Wyoming superintendent during playoff run

Oil City News
November 6, 2026
November 6, 2026

CASPER, Wyo. —
Steve Harshman won Tuesday’s election
to become Wyoming’s next superintendent of public instruction, but the longtime Natrona County High School football coach still has a playoff run to finish.
Within hours of the election call, Harshman was already looking ahead to the week’s football practice for tonight’s game between his undefeated Mustangs and the Cheyenne Central Indians at home in the postseason.
Harshman has spent decades coaching high school football and
serving in the Wyoming House of Representatives
as District 37’s Republican representative, including two stints as speaker of the House. While he prepares to take charge of the state’s education department, he first plans to finish what he started with his players.
“I’ll probably graduate with these seniors,” he said.
Head coach Steve Harshman gives a speech to his team after their victory during the Oil Bowl in October 2026. (Sydney Denekamp, Oil City News)
Harshman has balanced coaching and public service for most of his career. He campaigned across the state during the summer and primary season, but turned his full focus back to his players once school started in the fall. Balancing those roles hasn’t been a problem, he said.
Decades in the classroom and on the field bring rewards that have nothing to do with scoreboards, he said.
“It’s just so gratifying to see those kids grow up and be successful and doing really well,” Harshman told Oil City News. “In the education business, that’s what I call a paycheck.”
Extracurricular activities build character that lasts well beyond graduation, whether students pursue sports, music or other clubs, he added.
“It’s never been about the wins and losses,” he said. “If you’re in it for that, you know, it’s a pretty tough ride.”
Learning to take risks, handle disappointment and push through adversity when plans fall apart are the real lessons he works to pass along to students, he said.
Focus on classrooms
Harshman said he plans to carry that philosophy to the Wyoming Department of Education.
“It’s the foundation of our democracy,” he said of public education. While he commended Wyoming schools, he said improvement must continue.
Harshman said his administration will focus on providing direct, practical support to teachers, students and families.
“Wyoming is always in the top 10 of young adults who join the military. Our kids are patriots.”
Steve Harshman
Harshman said his team will prioritize putting literacy legislation passed by lawmakers into local classrooms. Turning state policy into classroom results is what matters most, he said.
It is a high school’s role to help students plan for life after graduation long before their senior year, he added. He said he wants to see students ready for adulthood without rushing them into rigid choices, whether they pursue college, vocational trades, apprenticeships or military service.
“Wyoming is always in the top 10 of young adults who join the military,” Harshman said. “Our kids are patriots.”
He also said he plans to open discussions on early childhood education, saying that Wyoming doesn’t fund a statewide prekindergarten program for 4-year-olds. He heard that concern repeatedly from voters on the campaign trail and said he plans to bring attention to early reading and childhood development.
School funding battles
Although the Legislature is tasked with recalibrating school funding every five years, until this year, lawmakers had not passed a recalibration bill since 2010. While Harshman served on five recalibration committees, he was not part of this year’s committee that passed Senate File 81.
That bill was enacted into law without Gov. Mark Gordon’s signature. In a letter to Senate President Bo Biteman, Gordon warned that the benefit of the bill’s teacher pay raises would come “at the expense of local flexibility and authority,”
WyoFile reported
.
Harshman said he
disagreed with several decisions
, including changes to how districts calculate student enrollment. He also criticized a regional cost adjustment that made teacher salaries in Casper less competitive than those in surrounding towns.
“I don’t think it passed the common-sense test,” Harshman said. “It says currently that it costs more to hire teachers in Riverton and Shoshoni than it does down the highway here in Casper. That really hurt Natrona County School District.”
Rep. Steve Harshman talks with Rep. Lloyd Larsen during the 2026 legislative session in Cheyenne. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)
Harshman also pushed back against legislative efforts to micromanage district spending, arguing that locally elected school boards need flexibility to make decisions for their communities.
“I think the Legislature stepped in where it shouldn’t be,” he said.
He also rejected claims by some lawmakers that districts diverted instructional money to administrative pay, saying financial reports show the overwhelming majority of funding goes directly to classroom teaching. He said he expects the incoming Legislature to correct those formulas.
Teacher appreciation, retention
Recruiting and keeping quality teachers is another hurdle for Wyoming schools. While compensation matters, Harshman said public recognition carries equal weight.
“The No. 1 motivator for all of us is appreciation,” he said.
Negative commentary on social media often drowns out the positive work happening in classrooms, he continued. As state superintendent, he said he wants to encourage districts to share ideas and successful programs, including career and technical education models and student internships.
Harshman said he wants to keep young residents from leaving Wyoming for opportunities elsewhere. That’s possible by expanding vocational training, certificate programs, dual enrollment and internships that help prepare students for careers and keep local talent at home, he said.
Investing for the future
The superintendent serves on several state boards that guide Wyoming’s long-term economic development. Harshman said Wyoming should focus on attracting employers that offer good pay rather than chasing rapid population growth.
The goal, he said, is giving young adults a reason to build their lives in Wyoming.
His personal motivation remains the same as he prepares for the transition into state leadership.
“I love the service of it,” he said.
It will take foresight from the state’s leaders to make it all possible, he added. The creation of the Permanent Wyoming Mineral Trust Fund in the 1970s is an example of that foresight that today’s policymakers must match, he said.
“What are we going to think up in 2026 that is going to make a difference in 2076? What am I going to do for the next generation to leave it better than we found it?” he asked. “That’s the goal and that’s the plan.”
He said success comes down to basic principles.
“There’s two things, only two things, that all of us can control, and that is our attitude and our effort,” he said, adding that what matters is how people face their circumstances and what they choose to give back.
For Harshman, leadership starts with meeting personal challenges and then helping others do the same. Moving from the football field to the Department of Education offers another opportunity to do just that.
“You soon figure out that by lifting others up, you lift yourself up,” he said. That principle, he added, matters far more than wins and losses.
