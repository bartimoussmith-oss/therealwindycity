Economic opportunities of Cheyenne’s new PRCA home ‘transformational’ for state, tourism experts say
by
Garrett Grochowski
October 22, 2026

(Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association’s move from Colorado Springs to Cheyenne could generate more than $250 million in economic impact in 10 years, Wyoming tourism experts predict.
The Wyoming Hospitality and Travel Coalition met in Sheridan on Oct. 22 for its annual fall summit. There, tourism experts like former Wyoming State Office of Tourism Director Diane Shober, current director Dominic Bravo and Visit Cheyenne CEO Jim Walter, prepared the rest of the state’s tourism leaders for the potential increase in tourism that Cheyenne’s PRCA facility may generate for the rest of the state.
Shober decribed the economic opportunities for Cheyenne as “transformational.”
The Wyoming Tourism Office expects a $252.6 million cumulative impact in the first 10 years the PRCA facility operates. That doesn’t count the economic activity driven by construction of the Hitching Post District, the Cheyenne business park slated to host the facility and the surrounding tourism complex. The district has a long way to go, as utility infrastructure installation hasn’t even begun yet.
The property was annexed into Cheyenne and zoned to accommodate the PRCA earlier this month.
The site of the Hitching Post District, which will eventually host the relocated Pro Rodeo Hall of Fame (City of Cheyenne)
The estimated annual economic impact from the Hall of Fame alone by year 10 of operation is expected to reach $12 million, Shober said.
The development is expected to generate 368 direct net jobs, with 140 jobs expected to come out of the PRCA facility itself. Those jobs would generate around $25.3 million in additional economic activity every year.
The development would also make Cheyenne a year-round destination, Shober and Walter agreed. The city already enjoys a regular influx of 175,000 visitors every July for Cheyenne Frontier Days; the presence of the PRCA facility would give them a reason to stay longer than the 2.2-day average and give visitors a reason to come to Wyoming during other seasons.
It also positions Wyoming to become the cultural home of rodeo, Shober said, which helps tourism across the state.
“It is one destination, but it will have a statewide ripple effect,” Shober said. “It’s a model for destination development, the work that you’re doing in all aspects, from small projects to large ones. This can be a poster child, model, a case study for what works, and even if there are things where we stumble, there will be things that we can learn from this. It’ll be a model for that destination development, in creating that flagship destination with statewide appeal.”
The PRCA oversees more than $1 billion a year in prize money, sanctions more than 800 events across 37 states and three Canadian provinces, and has 6,600 members and 35 million fans, Walter said. Rodeo enthusiasts from all over the continent are going to want to drive to see the facility.
“The question for every one of you in this room isn’t whether rodeo fans travel to Wyoming; it’s whether they’re traveling past or through your county, and how do you get them to stop there?” Walter said.
Visit Cheyenne President and CEO Jim Walter speaks at the Wyoming Hospitality and Travel Coalition’s fall summit Oct. 22, 2026 (Shawn Houck, Oil City News)
Wyoming already has the bones to become the rodeo state. Cody features the Nite Rodeo, Casper hosts the College National Finals Rodeo and Central Wyoming Fair and Rodeo, and Laramie hosts Jubilee Days. Wyoming already has more than 14 people inducted into the PRCA Hall of Fame. If Wyoming can better leverage its identity to be the rodeo state, it won’t be just Cheyenne benefiting from the PRCA facility.
“The economic impact doesn’t have to stop at the Laramie County line,” Walter said. “In fact, if it does, we fail.”
Walter compared what Cheyenne could be to Cooperstown, New York, which hosts the National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum. Cooperstown is just one stop on a series that visitors to the Hall of Fame make when visiting; they’ll spend their hard-earned dollars in nearby towns, driving tourism for the entire region.
The tourism experts imagined the ideal Wyoming road trip, where visitors coming from the south or east begin at the “gateway” to Wyoming in Cheyenne, stopping at the PRCA Hall of Fame or attending Cheyenne Frontier Days. Their trip ultimately takes them to the national parks on the opposite corner of the state, but they are tempted to stop at all the towns they drive through, where they’ll spend their money.
“The Hall of Fame doesn’t close the loop in Cheyenne,” Walter said. “It opens the rest of the state up to people who are never going to find it otherwise. The road doesn’t belong to us; it runs through all of you.”
