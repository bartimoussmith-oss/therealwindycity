CODY — Following last week’s federal elections, the U.S. Senate is set to take up a vote on a bill that could reform the laws governing the permitting of large energy and infrastructure projects.
The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, written and sponsored by Sens. Mike Lee, R-Utah; Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va; Martin Heinrich, D-N.M.; and Sheldon Whitehouse, D-R.I, would ease cumbersome federal regulations that allow for extensive delays or denial of permits. The bill also creates and updates the nation’s aging energy grid by ensuring the construction of new transmission lines.
This follows years of negotiation in which Congress has attempted to overhaul federal permitting, with policymakers, industry leaders and nonprofits agreeing the system requires reform to better expedite the construction of transmission lines, renewable and fossil fuel energy projects, mines and, of late, data centers.
The legislation has earned the support of Wyoming’s federal delegation, with Sen. John Barrasso, R-Wyo, saying he would “continue to fight to fix our broken permitting process, bring down energy costs, and create jobs across the country.”
“Wyoming energy workers are ready to produce the energy America needs,” he said in a statement provided to the Enterprise. “The federal permitting process is standing in their way. We need more energy now if we want to meet demand and keep it affordable for Wyoming families. Permitting reform is essential to doing that.”
During the last congressional session, Barrasso and Sen. Joe Manchin, I-W.Va, co-authored the Energy Permitting Reform Act of 2024. That bill, Barrasso said, earned strong bipartisan support in committee with a 15-4 vote; the new legislation, he added, builds on that work and serves as a compromise.
“We need permitting reform that ensures strong investment in energy and mineral production and responsible use of our resources on federal lands, while encouraging reasonable environmental review,” he said.
U.S. Sen.-elect Rep. Harriet Hageman, R-Wyo, was similarly supportive, saying that while the Senate is scheduled to act first on the bill, “I will continue to make the case for completing permitting reform in the 119th Congress.”
Hageman said she was “pleased (that) many of the proposed reforms to environmental laws, such as NEPA and the Clean Water Act, stem from House-passed bills, including the SPEED Act, which I was proud to co-lead with Chairman Westerman.”
However, the legislation has divided environmental advocates.
Some, including the Center of Western Priorities, have cited the bill’s provision barring environmental review as an example of the federal government attempting to fence Americans out of decisions regarding public lands.
Others, like the Bozeman-based Property and Environment Research Center, have expressed support for the reforms. McLean Albritton, a federal policy advisor for PERC, described a system that has become self-defeating: while these laws were crafted to protect the environment, Albritton said, the bureaucracy they created now hampers it.
“We’ve found that prescribed burns begin an average of five years after an environmental assessment, and seven and a half years after an environmental impact statement,” said Albritton.
Nine Tribal organizations, including the Rocky Mountain Tribal Leaders Council, which includes both the Northern Arapaho and Eastern Shoshone Tribal Councils, and the All Pueblo Council of Governors, have also expressed their opposition to the bill, saying Title III’s proposed changes “are in deep conflict with federal trust and treaty obligations.”
In an Oct. 9 letter submitted to Senate leaders and the bill’s authors, the Tribal Nations said the reviews required under Section 106 of the National Environmental Policy Act, which BAAJA would alter, “are vital to the continuity of Native cultures, the practice of our spiritual traditions, and the protection of our sacred sites from irreparable degradation and ruin.
“After centuries of federal action designed to strip us of these fundamental rights, the Section 106 review process, though imperfectly implemented, provides Tribal Nations with a mechanism and resources to help the federal government make better decisions that avoid the ongoing loss of our identities, ceremonies, and Ancestors in the name of ‘progress,” Tribal leaders wrote.
The White House has expressed support for the bill. In September, Reuters reported that President Donald Trump had told Democrats wind and solar energy projects would be given better treatment in the bill, accelerating its recent progress.
An industry handout
CWP, in a press release outlining the group’s opposition to the legislation, suggested that the bill would allow for more expeditious permitting of data centers on the country’s public lands.
Earlier this year, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum directed the Bureau of Land Management to identify land parcels for potential future siting. The Interior Department is already reviewing more than a dozen applications for data centers on public lands “and it attempted to approve the first one by skipping environmental review and public comment entirely.”
“If this bill passes, that could become the norm for permitting data centers on public lands,” according to a CWP press release.
The group said the bill would also shield permits for drilling and nuclear waste storage from future revocation by future administrations.
Tracy Stone-Manning, the Wilderness Society’s president and former director of BLM, said in a media release that a work-in-progress piece of legislation was an unsatisfactory solution to permitting problems.
“A clean energy future is possible and necessary, but not at the expense of our country’s conservation heritage, the clean water our public lands deliver, and the loss of cherished sacred sites and cultural histories,” she said, noting the risks posed by industrial development to watersheds and conservation efforts.
“Our country is capable of hard and great things. It can and must deliver clean energy, protected lands and tribal sovereignty to future generations. This particular attempt unfortunately fails to do so, and our elected officials should reject it,” she said.
Bureaucracy harms conservation work
PERC, however, has argued that the legal framework the bill would change hurts conservation efforts, and any alteration to the existing code would aid conservation work more than industry.
In an interview, Albritton recalled the Bozeman Municipal Watershed Project as a prime example of how activists could effectively delay a necessary project through environmental review and litigation. Under BAAJA, courts would no longer be able to pause projects for environmental review; instead, construction would continue while the reviews were performed. Challengers would have only 150 days to sue. At its core, he said that PERC supports the legislation for its reduction of duplicative analysis.
“It’s usually a few groups that challenge projects like this consistently,” Albritton added, noting that the changes would speed up projects that have clearly beneficial environmental impacts.
In the case of the watershed project, Albritton described it as a critical infrastructure project that was first proposed in 2005. The U.S. Forest Service sought to mitigate dense tree growth south of Bozeman through fuels-based and mechanical thinning, but the project was held up for 15 years by legal challenges and environmental review.
“We were lucky because if, at any point, a wildfire had broken out during that period of time, the Bozeman and Hyalite Creek drainages supply 80% of the city’s water,” he said.
Had the area burned, ash and sediment would have flowed into the city’s water treatment plant, potentially knocking it offline and contaminating the city’s water supply during an emergency situation.
While challenges often improve a project, Albritton said that any special interest group’s ability to delay projects for an extended time period ultimately does more harm than good.
The USFS, Albritton said, has around 80 million acres of federal forestlands “that need wildfire mitigation treatments.”
Those forests, he added, also contain expanding populations of invasive species, which threaten existing biodiversity as well as land that could provide habitat for endangered species. With a more streamlined permitting process, he said, it’s possible that habitat could be more easily remediated.
“Our economists’ research found that prescribed burns began an average of five-and-a-half years after an environmental assessment started and over seven years after an environmental impact statement,” said Albritton.
Similarly, PERC research determined that mechanical treatments begin, on average, between four and five years after a statement of intent.
The Breakthrough Institute, a Berkeley, California-based research center that identifies and promotes technological solutions to environmental and human development challenges, also signaled support for the legislation.
In an April blog post titled “The NEPA everyone is arguing about doesn’t exist anymore,” Marc Levitt, the director of environmental regulatory reform, said a deal “could codify requirements that never actually existed in the original statute, such as a requiring draft EISs for major projects, guaranteeing meaningful public comment tied to those documents, and aligning judicial standing with participation in that process.”
Indigenous perspective
Tribal leaders, however, criticized the bill for its reduction of judicial review. In the letter sent to the senators, leaders said that existing flaws within Section 106 already “result in failures to properly consult with Tribal Nations and to act upon our guidance.
“In the case of these failures, Tribal Nations are frequently left without recourse or options beyond the judicial system,” the leaders wrote. “We do not seek to engage in litigation, nor are these ‘nuisance’ lawsuits. Any litigation represents our last attempt at preserving our cultural identities. Instead of acknowledging and mitigating federal failures, this bill attempts to shut Tribal Nations out of the courtroom.”
Specifically, the Tribal Leaders said that BAAJA “nearly eliminates any real remedies available to Tribal Nations by stipulating remand without vacatur as a baseline, thus allowing projects to proceed while remand to address inadequate Section 106 review is ongoing.”
Tribal Nations, the letter concluded, “should not be forced to bear the brunt of these cultural and spiritual harms so the nation can further develop and transport its energy resources. We have already sacrificed the majority of our lands and natural resources — oftentimes through force or coercion — upon which the United States has become the world power it is today.”
