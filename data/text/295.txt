Proposed regulations to city construction sites pass committee reading Monday
by
Garrett Grochowski

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — After months of work by the Cheyenne Planning Commission, a handful of code changes affecting large construction sites have entered the municipal process. Passing the Public Services Committee 2–1 on its second reading, the ordinance introducing the changes will advance to the Cheyenne City Council.
The ordinance introduces a handful of amendments to the City of Cheyenne’s Unified Development Code, the master document for planning and development regulations for the city. Newly proposed changes would introduce strict property-line noise limits and construction site regulations while banning inefficient evaporative water cooling systems for future data center developments.
An amendment to one section would set construction noise limits aligned with
the Environmental Protection Agency’s health and sleep guidelines
. Sites adjacent to non-residential areas would not be permitted to produce more than 65 decibels, as recorded at their property lines, during the day and 60 decibels at night. Sites adjacent to residential neighborhoods are subject to even stricter regulations and may not produce any noise louder than 45 decibels during the day or 40 at night.
Consistent sounds exceeding the allowable limit would be considered illegal. If a complaint is filed, the site owner must provide verification using a special sound-level meter placed 5 feet above the ground and averaged over a five-minute period to demonstrate how many decibels the site is emitting.
Another newly proposed regulation would require contractors to submit a formal site layout detailing temporary structures, equipment yards, worker parking and internal haul routes. It would also mandate setbacks where construction staging areas meet neighborhoods — a 10-foot minimum setback from residential areas for lots less than an acre in size and a 50-foot minimum setback for anything larger.
Workforce housing areas are also subject to new buffer rules under this change. Temporary workforce housing like modular homes or RV sites must be set back at least 1,000 feet from any school, church or residential neighborhood.
Amendments to this section would also require that applicants post a financial bond, irrevocable letter of credit or cash escrow equal to 125% of an engineer’s estimated cost for site cleanup, regrading and revegetation. This also provides the community some degree of financial security if a project fails. It introduces rules for lighting as well, requiring temporary construction lighting to be fully shielded and directed downward to limit how much it creeps out.
Changes to another section of the UDC would impact how public utilities like the Board of Public Utilities work with large construction sites. If approved, changes to sections 1.1.8, 1.1.9, 4.5.3 and 4.5.4 would authorize BOPU and city staff to require “will serve” letters verifying water, sewer, electric and gas capacity before a site plan is approved; force site plans to report estimated monthly water usage in gallons; ban city staff from signing nondisclosure agreements and require that all development agreements be approved by the City Council.
Originally removed from consideration by the Planning Commission so that it could be worked out more thoroughly in a future meeting, a motion by committee member Mark Rinne also included changes to data center cooling.
Although already enforced by the BOPU since
a contractor at the Meta data center construction site discharged bacteria-infected water into the city’s wastewater treatment system this summer
, the amendment confirms that data centers cannot discharge construction fill-and-flush or glycol-impacted wastewater into the city’s sewer system without BOPU approval. It also officially prohibits inefficient evaporative cooling systems for future data centers, requiring the more environmentally-conscious closed-loop cooling systems.
Rinne, who sponsored the entire ordinance, said that the addition of the water regulations was the impetus for a whole batch of code changes. He said that most people within the city had already been saying that all new data centers were required to use closed-loop cooling, just to find out that one had been approved to use evaporative cooling just months before the requirement went into effect. This ordinance makes sure that there isn’t another way for city codes to be exploited again.
While that was being worked out, Rinne said, he and staff began adding other rules based on what they had heard over the past few months from city residents.
“This addresses all the things that we have been listening to from the public for months now. The purpose of the ordinance — you can say, ‘We need to discuss this longer, we need to work with this longer,’ that’s fine. You can discuss it for long as you want. But I would like to see some action,” Rinne said. “The action is based on what we have heard from the public over the last few months. I think this is a good start. As our staff says, it codifies what we are currently doing and just puts it into law how they’re currently handling policy.”
The ordinance did not earn the support of councilor Pete Laybourn, however. He said that trying to pass regulations for the largest industrial development period the city has ever seen in just a few months is hurried and inadequate.
“It is premature. It is really something that requires a lot more understanding, both on the part of ourselves and I think the construction community and particularly all the different data centers and where their participation in this occurs,” Laybourn said. “This is the most massive industrial development this city has ever seen. It is absolutely without comparison.”
The Planning Commission and city staff, responsible for making recommendations on changes and for drafting the code changes, respectively, are well-intentioned but probably unprepared for interpreting or enforcing highly technical industrial standards, he said.
“We should have better information,” Laybourn said. “We should have had better information as we began to go down this road. I’m pleased that we’re doing better with some of the projects that are coming up and the development agreements that might happen, but a really in-depth understanding that certainly could have been acquired some time ago when we knew that this was occurring just didn’t happen. I believe that this is inadequate.”
Fixing it later isn’t the same as being prepared from the beginning, he said. The city should work more with developers regarding the operational realities of their facilities before locking in mandates, he concluded.
The ordinance was ultimately voted on favorably by the majority of the committee, however, advancing it to the Cheyenne City Council for a reading on Nov. 9. If it passes a reading there, the committee will look it over one more time before the City Council takes a final vote.
