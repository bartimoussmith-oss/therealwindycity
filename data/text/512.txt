Proposed wind project southeast of Cheyenne gets Laramie County Planning support

The Laramie County Planning Commission meets Nov. 12, 2026 (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — An energy development company’s request for a permit with Laramie County to develop a wind project will head to the Board of County Commissioners next week after earning the Planning Commission’s recommendation Thursday. If the project is eventually fully approved, the company will develop a 5,000-acre energy development between Laramie County and Weld County, Colorado.
Project representatives spoke to the Laramie County Planning Commission during its Nov. 12 meeting asking that the board, which makes land use–related recommendations to the Laramie County commissioners, support its application for a Class C Conditional Use Permit. A Class C permit is the most stringent, requiring thorough review by staff, the Planning Commission and the county commissioners. It also requires that developers submit more thorough plans ahead of construction and participate in multiple public hearings.
What representatives from
Enyo
, the developer behind the project, and consulting firm
Logan Simpson
told the commission is that Laramie County will not experience much of an impact from this project.
“The project is 12 wind turbines,” Cindy Delancey, a Cheyenne lawyer representing Enyo, told the commission. “It’s the smallest wind project to ever be proposed in Laramie County.”
The development,
according to the project website
, promises to deliver up to 500 megawatts of wind energy, 500 megawatts of solar energy, and 350 megawatts of natural gas reciprocating engines, and will feature a battery energy storage system and a large network of transmission cables and substations to get the power to where it needs to go.
Weld County is slated to host most of that infrastructure, including all of the solar panels and batteries. Laramie County will host 12 of the turbines, infrastructure like access roads and lay-down yards and miles of transmission lines, which run to Cheyenne to provide power to the city.
The need for that power, Delancey said, has never been greater.
“We have a unique situation in Laramie County in that the local demand for energy continues to outpace supply,” Delancey said. “Cheyenne and Black Hills Energy have been faced with power constraints for many years.”
This project, she continued, can be the solution. That got the support of Wyoming House District 44 Rep. Lee Filer, who told the commission that the state is already suffering from energy constraints.
“We have mining operations that cannot get power right now. We have manufacturing companies [that] are trying to come here but they can’t because we don’t have enough power and resources for that,” Filer said. “It’s not just because of the data centers. It’s because of the actual growth we’re seeing in Wyoming. Here in Wyoming, we do believe that strong economic growth in our county and our state is a good thing we’ve been asking for for years.”
While the increasing number of data centers in and around Cheyenne certainly exacerbates the need for power, the stress that Enyo’s project takes off the local grid is beneficial wholesale, Jeremy Call of Logan Simpsons told the commission. As an environmental planner, he commended the renewable aspects of the project and the nonintrusive nature of turbines versus other energy developments.
“Critics might argue that any form of development will destroy this area, but let’s remember that energy development is temporary and at the end of the useful life, everything will be decommissioned,” Call said.
That is important, he said, because of the agricultural value of the area. Eleven miles southeast of Cheyenne, the area falls outside the borders of PlanCheyenne and has staved away most forms of energy development since southern Laramie County began booming as an industrial area. Forty years as a wind project before an unobtrusive decommissioning would still allow cattle grazing.
“This is an area for agriculture and agriculture will continue; the cattle ranching that is there today will continue,” Call said. “The permanent disturbance is only 0.2% of the 5,000 acres — roughly 11 acres of permanent disturbance.”
However, what the project’s neighbors, like Nicholas Merz, are concerned about is the visual impact. Merz wrote in letters to the county and told the commission Thursday that he and his family love their property in southeast Laramie County for its rural character.
“My family and our livelihood will be permanently impacted by the Chalk Bluffs Energy Project,” Merz said. “This county talks about personal property rights. However, it appears that is the case only for some and not for all. I bought my land for the bluffs, the views, the open horizon, the night sky. It’s our retirement, my sons’ inheritance. Their futures are in jeopardy with this.”
Having experienced the encroachment of industry up close, however, King Ranch owner Mark Eisele, whose property has been leasing to wind energy for years and is adjacent to
the future Cox Ranch data center
, said that wind is about as good an offer as it gets.
“I’d like to offer a different perspective,” Eisele said, stating that he had no stake in the outcome of the permit. “The King Ranch is basically in the center of the hurricane. You’ve got a data center, you’ve got a wind farm, you’ve got solar, you’ve got a chemical plant — we’ve got a bit of everything. The least intrusive, the least troublesome of them all, are the wind farms.”
They’ve been a “boon” to the ranching family, he said, which reaches back multiple generations. In the 17 years that turbines have been on their land, they’ve reportedly experienced little negative impact, including bird strikes.
“And it’s really been no detriment to our cattle,” he continued.
The Planning Commission ultimately recommended granting the permit to the Laramie County Board of Commissioners. However, they attached a mandatory condition that all state and local agency review comments — including Wyoming Game & Fish monitoring plans and Public Works road agreements — must be fully resolved prior to final site plan approval. Call did note in his presentation to the Planning Commission that Game & Fish in particular commended the project for its minimal impact on wildlife.
The commissioners will ultimately be responsible for granting the permit and later approving a site plan. The commissioners will hear the request on Nov. 17 during their industrial quarterly meeting. They’ll hold a second public hearing before making a determination on the permit Dec. 1. According to representatives, Enyo is seeking site plan approval Dec. 15.
