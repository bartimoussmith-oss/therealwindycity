New school boundary lines drawn at Laughlin Road ahead of Jessup Elementary closure

Jessup Elementary banner in LCSD1's Storey Gym meeting room (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The Laramie County School District 1 Board of Trustees has officially decided where to draw the new school boundaries following the closure of Jessup Elementary school next year. The trustees’ final decision was not one they originally considered, but they selected it based on parent feedback.
Jessup Elementary, serving students in the Central Triad west of Interstate 25 and east of F.E. Warren Air Force Base,
is the third of eight local schools slated for closure following the findings of a 2024 district cost effectiveness study
. Just like with Miller and Deming elementaries, one important consideration the district was faced with is where to send students the school originally served.
At the end of this school year, the last class of Jessup Jaguars will leave for summer break. Following the board’s final vote on the matter Nov. 16, neighbors along
Laughlin Road
who originally sent their children to the same school will now be sending them to different schools based on what side of the street their house is on.
District administration originally proposed three different ways to split up the neighborhoods that once sent their kids to Jessup. Their favorite, and the one they were planning to recommend to the trustees, “squared up” the boundaries best and sent kids to either Davis Elementary or Prairie Wind Elementary based on what side of
Brittany Drive
they lived on.
(Laramie County School District 1)
However, that proposed boundary split up a tight-knit neighborhood, District Superintendent Stephen Newton said after
hearing community feedback in recent weeks
.
“We heard from board members and community members, we had folks at our Jessup community meeting that said perhaps we have an interest beyond just squaring up geometrically our boundaries,” Newton said. “And there was the idea that there are a lot of residential areas right in that area that would be cut through with this particular option. And so an alternative was offered.”
They elected to split the neighborhood along Laughlin Road. Although it split a neighborhood just as the Brittany option did, Newton said, this option would keep more students together.
“It would keep all of those students who were classmates with each other at Jessup from Laughlin on south pulled over into the Davis boundary,” Newton said. “This would be an option that values keeping a cohort of kids together in the same school.”
(Laramie County School District 1)
That proposal was ultimately what the trustees approved. Students living in Jessup’s historic boundaries but either south of Laughlin drive west of I-25 or south of Four Mile Road east of I-25 will go to Davis next year. Kids on the north side will attend Prairie Wind. For both schools, capacity is not a concern.
Parents who aren’t satisfied with the board’s results can apply for a boundary waiver that, if approved, would allow them to send their kid elsewhere. Newton confirmed in a meeting with Jessup parents Nov. 6 that Jessup families would have first priority when waiver applications are considered near the end of the school year.
The acceptance of a waiver application is partly dependent on whether the desired school is at full capacity. Additionally, students attending schools outside their area due to a boundary waiver can’t use district buses; their parents would have to drive them.
The only consideration the district has left regarding Jessup Elementary is what to do with the building once it is clear of students. Historically, those decisions are made later, during the summer following closure.
There are a few fates the district could decide on, but Newton said there isn’t anything in particular that administration is seeking. If the building is in good shape and the district finds a need, it could be used for some administrative purpose. Miller Elementary, for example, is now used as a storage facility. Deming Elementary, which the district couldn’t find a use for but which was added to the
National Register of Historic Places in 2005
and could therefore not easily be demolished, was put up for sale.
District administration said it believes the board has made a decision that works for students and thoughtfully considered parents’ wishes.
“The feedback process worked,” Newton said.
