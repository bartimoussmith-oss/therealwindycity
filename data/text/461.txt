Crushed in governor’s race, Bextel may be in hot water in Jackson over ‘15 illegal chickens’

JACKSON — Rebecca Bextel apparently has 15 “illegal chickens.”
That’s what the East Jacksonite and unsuccessful gubernatorial candidate told conservative commentator Tucker Carlson during a chummy conclusion to her appearance on Carlson’s show Oct. 24.
Bextel also questioned the moon landing and made other false or misleading statements in the wide-ranging, pre-election interview.
“The governor’s mansion is going to turn into a hen house,” Bextel told Carlson.
Bextel, running for the Equality State’s top elected office on the Constitution Party ticket, was defeated in the general election. The Associated Press called the race at 7 p.m., the minute polls closed. Bextel had earned a little less than 17% of the statewide vote.
Chicken-rearing is, indeed, illegal within town limits, although the Jackson Town Council tangled over allowing the practice in 2024. Jackson Mayor Arne Jorgensen said he found Bextel’s comments troubling.
“Rebecca ... backs up against the U.S. Forest Service. She lives in the area that is the highest risk for a wildlife engagement,” Jorgensen said. “For her to be so flippant about that is highly concerning.”
Whether the town will enforce its regulations against Bextel’s birds remains to be seen.
The Jackson Police Department is tasked with enforcing the rule. The department typically enforces the animal regulation when it receives a complaint, according to Jorgensen and Lieutenant Russ Ruschill. The town does not have the resources to have code enforcement officers roving town streets on the lookout for urban farmers.
The police department would not seize chickens without a judge telling officials to do so, Ruschill said.
Town officials had not received a complaint about Bextel’s chickens as of Friday, Ruschill said.
“I didn’t watch the Tucker Carlson interview,” the lieutenant said.
Bextel did not return a request for comment for this article. Carlson also did not return a request for comment.
Fair framing
Bextel told Carlson that the town does not allow backyard chickens because of the environment. She did not elaborate.
The town likewise does not allow honeybees or fruit trees, but Bextel also said she has fruit trees.
Carlson was aghast and said he thought the rule was in place to deprive Jacksonites of “auxiliary food sources.”
“The fact they banned fruit trees, honeybees, and chickens in the name of nature is pretty remarkable,” he said. “They’re going to be ... blowing up the Tetons to put in data centers in the name of the environment at some point.”
But that framing misses the context, said Kristin Combs, executive director of Wyoming Wildlife Advocates. Jackson Hole sits at the doorstep of the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem, one of the largest intact ecosystems on earth, where grizzly bears occasionally walk through town, Combs said.
That means people have to take their environment into account and behave differently.
“We live in a special place. We have bears. We need to behave in a certain way to make sure that bears are still able to be present here and people are staying safe at the same time,” Combs said.
When people and bears have conflict, both can lose, Combs said. People can suffer injury or property damage, and bears can get hooked on human food, forcing wildlife managers to either move or kill bears.
Changing chicken rules
In 2024, the Jackson Town Council looked into legalizing in-town chickens. The town council also considered allowing chickens in 2014 but chose not to because of concerns about the amount of time and money it would take to draft a law and regulate the practice.
After several citizens and agencies — including Wyoming Game and Fish — urged the council not to allow chickens two years ago, the town council opted to retain its existing rules. Then-Mayor Hailey Morton Levinson had come out in favor of chickens but later reversed course.
Game and Fish’s Jackson region wildlife management coordinator Cheyenne Stewart told councilors in a letter at the time that bears had been involved in 29 conflicts in the last decade in incidents involving chicken coops and beehives. Four bears — three black bears and one grizzly — were captured or euthanized because of the spats.
“Grizzly bear 399 and her offspring have been involved in eight conflicts involving chicken coops and beehives,” Stewart said in her letter. The famed grizzly died shortly thereafter, struck by a commuter in the Snake River Canyon.
Even if the town council had allowed for in-town chickens, Bextel’s backyard flock likely would have exceeded allowable limits. Town staffers had suggested a six-chicken maximum.
