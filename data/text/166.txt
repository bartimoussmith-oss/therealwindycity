HISTORY: Wyoming rededicates Spanish-American War Memorial after years in storage
by
Garrett Grochowski
October 29, 2026
October 29, 2026

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — Fourteen Wyoming soldiers lost their lives during the Spanish-American War, the short 1898 conflict between the United States and Spain sparked by the sinking of the USS Maine in the Havana Harbor in Cuba. Most of them, Adjutant General of Wyoming Major General Porter said, had probably never left the state of Wyoming before they sailed the Pacific to the jungles of the Philippines.
In 1905, they’d be honored with the dedication of a memorial statue raised on the Capitol Complex grounds. Originally financed by the Wyoming Volunteer Aid Society, a volunteer group of dedicated Wyoming women, the idea to honor the men who fought for Wyoming in the violent 10-week war was eventually supported by the 1899 Wyoming legislature and became reality.
The idea to immortalize those soldiers was realized in the same building where the soldiers took their oath of enlistment under the capitol rotunda. The memorial, featuring the likeness of a soldier from Waco, Texas, who had fought in the Philippines, went up just meters away outside the capitol building.
This undated photo of Wyoming’s Spanish-American War Memorial “Taking the Oath” on the Capitol grounds was taken by J.E. Stimson. The main inscription reads, “Created to the memory of the heroes of the Spanish-American War by the State of Wyoming and the Ladies Volunteer Aid Society.” (Courtesy Wyoming State Archives and the Wyoming Legislature)
The statue was moved around the capitol complex multiple times in the next century, slowly falling apart each time. It had even been reinstalled incorrectly after one move: A part of its base faced in the opposite direction it had been designed to. In the 1990s, it was also the victim of spray-paint vandalism. After a costly cleanup, the paint was gone, but the aging of the statue was apparent.
Talks of restoring the statue began in the early 2000s, but went nowhere. At that time, it was estimated to cost nearly $12,000 to bring the memorial back to its old self.
In 2018, during the four-year project to restore and renovate the Wyoming Capitol Complex, the statue was completely disassembled and removed from the grounds. It was during its time in storage that it finally underwent extensive repairs. After years outside, parts of its fingers were missing and it had chips, scratches and discoloration in multiple places.
(Photo courtesy of the State of Wyoming Legislature)
Though the capitol renovation eventually wrapped up, the memorial remained archived. According to Wyoming National Guard Historical Society’s Rosalind Schliske, most people had even lost track of the statue for a few years. She and Retired Colonel Larry Barttelbort eventually tracked its pieces to one of the Wyoming State Museum’s offsite storage facilities in late 2023. Desperate to get the statue back up as soon as possible, they quickly managed to get funding for the reinstallation slipped into the state’s 2024 supplemental budget.
After so many moves, though, they weren’t exactly sure where the statue ought to go upon its return. It took the insight of one of the National Guard Historical Society’s longest-tenured members and previously lost photographs to determine that its rightful home was to the east of the capitol building near the Wyoming State Museum, facing east towards the Philippines.
Now in 2026, the memorial is back where it belongs. The Wyoming State Museum, the Wyoming National Guard Historical Society, Wyoming Gov. Mark Gordon and other state and military officials and veterans gathered around the statue’s new home Oct. 29 for a special rededication.
Retired Colonel Larry Barttelbort speaks at the Oct. 29, 2026, rededication ceremony for the Spanish-American War Memorial in Cheyenne (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
The Spanish-American War Memorial in Cheyenne (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
Gordon said that, through the memorial, Wyoming has made a powerful statement about what makes the United States of America the special place it is — it looks forward to “a future which remembers the past.”
“The war was very short, but Americans and Wyoming people were willing, as we always are, to step up to make sure that we defend our country, our honor and the independence and liberties we so value,” Gordon said. “But more than just memorials, Wyoming always stands to remember our veterans as well.”
Wyoming Gov. Mark Gordon speaks at the Oct. 29, 2026, rededication ceremony for the Spanish-American War Memorial in Cheyenne (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
That sentiment was shared by Adjutant General of Wyoming Greg Porter, who said that remembering soldiers, even the ones more than 100 years removed from the new recruits of 2026, connects through time the greater purpose of military service.
“I swear I will support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic. That oath connects all of us throughout the years, the decades, to one another. I hope it connects maybe those that have not served, but know why we serve: for you. That connection is what keeps it real for us. Whatever we feel in the presence of this statue, folks after it will feel the same thing. And there’s a certain solace in that that those feelings will continue across the ages,” Porter said, looking upwards towards the steely gaze of the soldier. “He’s finally home now.”
Attendees at the Oct. 29, 2026, rededication ceremony for the Spanish-American War Memorial in Cheyenne (Garrett Grochowski, Cap City News)
The rededication was also a big win for the Wyoming National Guard Historical Society and the Wyoming State Museum. Schliske and Bartellbort have been working for years to get the statue back where it belongs. It seemed fitting to Schliske that she spoke to the hard work of the women 121 years ago who made sure the memorial existed in the first place.
Now, honoring both the men who fought in the Spanish-American War and the women who fought to have those soldiers recognized, the memorial is back. Standing proudly outside the
Wyoming State Museum
, it watches east for perpetuity.
Porter left the ceremony’s audience with a challenge.
“Bring someone here, a child, grandchild, a neighbor. Stand where we are standing. Point up to that soldier. Tell them about the volunteers who took the oath and the families to remember them,” Porter said. “A memorial only lasts while we’re here to remember it. Honor that oath. remember that sacrifice.”
